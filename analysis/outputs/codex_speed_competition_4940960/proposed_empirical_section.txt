3.4. Empirical Implications

The advancement of speed technology has become one of the most salient developments in
recent securities trading. This trend has driven a rapid rise in machine-driven trading,
particularly characterized by speed. Empirically, extensive discussions have addressed factors
that could influence stock market price informativeness. This paper outlines several testable
empirical implications.

Prediction 1. (Speed competition and price informativeness). i) Short-run price
informativeness increases with speed competition; ii) There exists a hump-shaped relationship
between speed competition and long-run price informativeness.
                                                                                              □
     Existing empirical studies appear to provide indirect support for these predictions. For
example, short-term (intraday) measures of price efficiency (e.g., order flow predictability,
serial correlation) seem to improve (Brogaard et al., 2014; Hirschey, 2018), while long-term
price informativeness seems negatively affected (e.g., Weller, 2018). To formally test these
theoretical predictions, we would need clear measures of both price informativeness and speed

      Sample Construction: The main sample could include the U.S.-listed companies trading
on the NYSE, NASDAQ, and AMEX, with firm-level financial statement data drawn from
Compustat, supplemented by intangible capital estimates from Peters and Taylor (2017) in
WRDS. Stock price information could be sourced from CRSP. Since this study focuses on
strategic traders, metrics of speed competition might be constructed by extracting institutional
holdings data from Thomson Reuters 13F and gathering fund strategies from the SEC’s
EDGAR database. Using these data, variables such as fast and slow institutional ownership
concentration could be created for each firm-quarter.
      For firm-level empirical tests, we might select the most recent ownership variables relative
to each firm’s fiscal year-end and then merge them with firm-level financial data. Sampling
criteria would exclude firms with a stock price below $1 or a market cap below $500 million,
as well as firms in the financial industry and those with fewer than four years of continuous
accounting data. We could further require that sample firms have at least one institutional
investor. For metrics that rely on firm-level concentration, requiring a minimum of five active
institutional investors could prevent extreme values. Unless otherwise specified, these criteria
would apply consistently throughout all the proposed analyses.

      Measures of Speed Competition: Classifying institutions as fast or slow investors could
follow Abis (2022) machine learning-based classification. This method uses data from the
"Principal Investment Strategies" (PIS) section of mutual fund prospectuses, part of the N-1A
filing since December 1999.11 In this proposed procedure, a random sample of PIS sections
would be reviewed by expert classifiers, with their classifications used to train a machine
learning algorithm—a random forest—to categorize institutions as fast or slow. This algorithm
is transparent and interpretable, providing a ranked list of features (e.g., keywords and
combinations) that inform classification.
