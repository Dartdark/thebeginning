## 1. What is it about?

A tutorial on time-series momentum: take positions in the direction of an asset's past return, spread exposure
across futures, and adjust leverage for estimated volatility. It covers why trends might persist, the importance
of the formation horizon, equivalent trend filters, causal signal lag, diversification, leverage and crisis behavior.
The authors illustrate moving-average trading on simulated returns, reproduce results from earlier papers and
provide a Chinese futures portfolio study. Those are different evidence sources, not one new live strategy.

The original empirical universe comprises 36 Chinese domestic futures, expanding as contracts become available
(pp. 29–30). Inputs begin June 30, 1997; reported trading history is June 30, 1999–June 28, 2019. This includes
stock indices, government bonds, metals, energy/minerals, industrial and agricultural markets.

## 2. What does it find?

The original Chinese study's Table 6 (p. 35) reports:

| Author portfolio | Annual return | Annual volatility | Maximum drawdown | Reported Sharpe |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1-month trend | 14.32% | 20.90% | −42.42% | 0.69 |
| 3-month trend | 16.23% | 20.95% | −30.29% | 0.77 |
| 12-month trend | 15.80% | 24.15% | −45.26% | 0.65 |
| Equal-weight combination | **16.24%** | **18.51%** | **−22.63%** | **0.88** |
| Equal-weight buy-and-hold | 2.53% | 14.12% | −45.24% | 0.18 |

These are **author-reported historical figures**, not our backtests. The paper does not supply a transaction-cost
schedule or a net-cost replication of Table 6. Nor does it identify a chronological design/untouched validation
split for this study. Its out-of-sample discussion on p. 38 summarizes other researchers' criticisms; that does
not turn its own 1999–2019 sample into out-of-sample validation. The 2025 version contains no 2020+ Chinese result.

Other distinctions that matter:

- The 13/50-day long-only SMA experiment (pp. 9–16) uses artificial return processes: 5,000 paths for each of nine
  drift/autocorrelation settings. It is a simulation illustration, not market evidence for a new crossover.
- Table 2 (p. 22), including the 1.45 Sharpe for 260-day TSMOM, is explicitly reproduced from Levine and Pedersen
  (2016), not the authors' independent US futures replication. EMA parameters there are centers of mass, not
  ordinary platform EMA periods; copying the numbers as periods would change the filters.
- The source reports positive trend returns in five of seven Chinese equity drawdown episodes, with about 60%
  in 2008 (p. 27). This is retrospective crisis-period evidence, not proof that a hedge pays before an Apex breach.
- One-third of the individual assets have negative annual returns in the combined-horizon study (p. 34).
  Broad diversification is part of the claim; selecting only the winning contracts would change the experiment.

Our independent checks here are limited to source inspection and arithmetic of published numbers.
16.24/18.51 ≈ 0.877 and 2.53/14.12 ≈ 0.179 round to the reported Sharpes. This does not reproduce the return
series, identify the Sharpe risk-free/mean convention or independently verify profitability.
The combination carries about **31% more realized volatility** than buy-and-hold; that benchmark is not risk
matched. The stated approximate 12% portfolio-volatility objective (p. 33) corresponds to 18.51% realized in
Table 6—about **54% higher**, not evidence of a guaranteed portfolio-risk cap.

## 3. Why does it make money, if it does?

The proposed mechanism (pp. 3–4) is gradual adjustment to information: anchoring/disposition effects initially
slow price discovery, followed by herding and eventual reversal. Diversification across persistent trends can
improve a basket's risk-adjusted performance. These are proposed explanations, not an identified forced flow,
observable order schedule or demonstrated causal mechanism in this study.

The paper itself presents competing evidence (p. 38): volatility scaling and persistent asset-average returns
may explain part of apparent momentum profits. A scaled buy-and-hold or past-only historical-mean-sign baseline
is therefore necessary in a future justified experiment. Comparing a risk-scaled strategy only with an unscaled
long basket does not isolate directional prediction. Cash collateral yield and roll cashflows also need accounting.

Long-horizon convexity is not an option contract or guaranteed loss cap. The authors acknowledge continuous-price
and restrained-leverage assumptions (p. 5); gaps, abrupt reversal and prolonged whipsaw remain material risks.
Their crisis evidence does not establish protection over our evaluation/funded books' intraday loss horizons.

## 5. Can a strategy code be made?

**Yes, the mathematical monthly rule is codeable.** Equations 18–20 (pp. 33–34) specify, at month end, the sign of
each asset's trailing 1-, 3- or 12-month return; multiply next month's return by that sign and 40% divided by
estimated annualized asset volatility; average across eligible assets and then across the three horizons.
Inverse volatility appears once in Equation 20; do not double-apply it because the prose also discusses weighting.

An executable implementation still needs causal month-end availability/next-fill timing, the EWM decay and
warm-up, point-in-time eligible universe, native contracts/rolls, collateral, margin, integer positions and complete
transaction costs. A monthly signal does not authorize a fill at the close that first reveals it. A research
signal calculator could be built; a faithful executable reproduction is not determined by the supplied settings.
No new strategy or MultiCharts code was written because the novelty/account-fit gate did not admit a test.
The committed audit script only reads source text, prior saved output and descriptive metadata.
