## 1. What is it about?

The paper argues that correcting selection bias and establishing a historical average return do not establish that the same conditions will persist. It contrasts false discoveries with missed discoveries, criticizes overreliance on bootstrap/parameter perturbation and significance metrics, and favors mechanism-led hypotheses and a smaller search space.

It contains actual illustrative trading rules, not merely organizational theory: a long-only price-above-moving-average strategy, and a long-only rule buying after two nonpositive daily returns and exiting after two positive returns. These are historical S&P 500 daily examples with potentially multi-day holds, hypothetical index shares and $0.01/share commission. They are not tested NQ/ES futures implementations.

## 2. What does it find?

The source reports a change in the relative performance of these rules between 1950–1997 and 1998–May 2016:

| Example | Earlier-period Sharpe | Later-period Sharpe | Evidence status |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| Daily moving average, m=11 | 1.23 | −0.13 | Author's Tables 1–2; not our market-data replication |
| Two-down/two-up reversal | −0.04 | 0.47 | Author's Tables 3–4; other table quantities need reconciliation |

The m=11 moving average is selected as median Sharpe among 19 lookbacks, 2–20 days. The later-period moving-average drawdown is reported as 55.2%. The paper relates the contrast to a rolling 252-day return-autocorrelation change near 1997, and shows monthly SPY/EEM illustrations. This is descriptive historical evidence, not a frozen real-time regime classifier or a randomized test that autocorrelation caused the change. The historical split's selection provenance is not established by the upload.

### Source arithmetic checked

The audit runs only the paper's supplied numbers and toy sequences; it does not test new market returns.

- **Path example, p. 3:** both ten-return sequences have seven +10% and three −10% returns. They share mean 4%, sample standard deviation about 9.661%, and final compounded wealth about 1.420615 per initial unit. With initial wealth included in the peak, their maximum drawdowns are **10% and 27.1%**. The source's approximately 33% second drawdown does not match this conventional calculation. The useful qualitative path-dependence point survives.
- **Bootstrap, pp. 7–8/Figure 1:** quoted mean 0.00468 divided by quoted sampling SD 0.00895 equals **0.522905**, conflicting with “more than 7 standard deviations.” Figure 1 visually repeats SD 0.00895; this is not merely a text-extraction error. The histogram scale also needs reconciliation. No corrected SD, significance claim or p-value is invented.
- **Table 3, p. 11:** annualized return −0.038, annualized volatility 0.098 and Sharpe −0.04 do not reconcile through a simple return/volatility ratio. CAGR and arithmetic-return definitions can differ, so this alone does not establish which entry is wrong; the source does not resolve the discrepancy. Its market-performance numbers remain unverified.

## 3. Why does it make money, if it does?

There is no newly verified source of profit here. The proposed explanations are persistence for trend following and reversal after short-term losses for mean reversion. A past profitable example is not evidence that either effect remains executable today at futures costs.

The sound inference is **historical significance does not guarantee future stationarity**. A false-positive test and a future regime failure are different questions: a correctly detected positive mean in one period can genuinely disappear later. Likewise, rejecting a losing rule in an earlier period is not automatically a Type II error for that period's mean. The paper's Type I/II language sometimes conflates statistical error with failure to predict a changed future distribution.

Multiple-testing adjustment, power analysis and risk simulation still serve distinct useful purposes. No counterexample makes those safeguards unnecessary. Correlated strategy variants require dependence-aware inference; they do not erase the selection history. No statistical procedure guarantees survival through an arbitrary, previously unseen regime.

## 4. Can we build on it?

**Yes as a validation reminder; neither illustrative strategy earns a new test from this paper.** Retain:

1. State what each result addresses: signal/selection evidence, chronological account survival, implementation fidelity, or ongoing stability. Passing one does not answer all four.
2. Describe bootstrapped payouts and drawdowns as conditional on the empirical history, dependence model, costs and rules. Shuffling old outcomes does not generate an unseen structural break. Block bootstrap preserves some observed clustering; it does not solve nonstationarity.
3. Keep mechanism-led, preregistered research and existing frozen drift/forward checkpoints. A proposed autocorrelation detector, regime filter or trend-to-reversal switch is a **new fitted strategy**, requiring past-only information, selection/trial accounting and out-of-sample controls. A plot annotated after observing the break is insufficient.
4. Evaluate path risk at actual integer size. Identical average/Sharpe statistics do not imply identical intraday floor breaches, payout timing or survival. The toy audit validates arithmetic only, not a particular Apex failure rate.
5. Limit the search before outcomes, while retaining rejected variants and counting all searches. Repeatedly selecting a narrow family after inspecting results can still mine the data; highly correlated predictors are not automatically portfolio diversification.

Repository overlap: `RESEARCH_CONTROLS.md`, the evidence/power template, block resampling, intraday account simulations, frozen forward log and drift monitoring already address much of this. `TIER_LIST.md` also records unsuccessful regime/Markov and 20-day-state filters. This paper does not justify rerunning those definitions or selecting favorable regimes afterward.

### Why no new backtest

The examples provide no identified forced flow or independently evidenced modern futures risk premium. The source's same-closing-price execution is not supported by a disclosed decision/order clock or trade ledger; a causal next-price implementation would need specification. Nontradeable index prices, hypothetical-share commission, multi-day exposure and unresolved numerical claims prevent treating the tables as a modern NQ/ES replication target. Holding these rules across sessions also conflicts with ordinary Apex flat-by-15:59 requirements. Converting them to intraday rules would define new strategies rather than reproduce this paper.

The smallest justified next action is retaining the methodology lesson. An exact replication would first need reconciled source quantities, original data/code and causal execution/cost definitions; no data purchase or return study is warranted solely to illustrate a limitation our existing process already recognizes.
