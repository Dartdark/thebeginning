## Rule (as tested)

No return rule was tested. Source: Hilary Till and Joseph Eagleeye,
*Commodity Futures Trading Strategies: Trend-Following and Calendar Spreads*, January17 2017,
[uploaded full text](../inputs/till_2942340_20261031/paper.pdf), SSRN2942340, five pages.

**What is it about?** Three practices: diversified medium/long-term trend following; calendar spreads linked to
seasonal inventories; and calendar spreads anticipating commodity-index rolls. Trend horizons discussed are
one to six months across many markets, rather than an intraday entry signal.

**What does it find?** This is a practitioner explanation with cited examples, not an original empirical strategy
study. The natural-gas curve is a June28 2016 snapshot; storage seasonality averages1994–2015. Neither chart
demonstrates achievable trading returns. The cited estimate that over70% of managed-futures funds use trends
is a prevalence claim, not a win rate. The article gives no trade ledger, cost study, test split or confidence interval.

**Why does it make money, if it does?** The paper proposes that hedgers and index investors can pay a price
concession to counterparties. A scheduled roll sells one maturity and buys another; an anticipator can establish
short-front/long-deferred exposure beforehand and unwind into that flow. This is plausible pressure, not a verified
profit here. Seasonal physical storage can lock in a margin only when the operator owns capacity, handles delivery
and covers financing/storage costs. A futures-only trader lacks that replication. Trend returns and useful crisis
diversification are discussed, not demonstrated for our book; a label does not certify a hedge.

**Can we build on it?** Retain the index-roll spread idea as conditional personal research. It identifies the
participant and direction more clearly than a generic indicator. It supplies neither a complete protocol nor matching
paired price data, so it does not earn a backtest now. Existing broad commodity trend research is not new because
this article describes it. Do not create a daily-flat NQ/ES adaptation from a multiday wheat/NG example.

## What could make this wrong

The source illustrates an economic mechanism without demonstrating a current net return. Index mandates,
replacement contracts, roll weights and anticipatory participation change. A published schedule is not proof
of residual surprise; a trader can also lose when index flow is anticipated. Seasonal averages are not current
as-of inventory forecasts. Delivery scarcity and weather can move the spread adversely even if outright delta
partly offsets. Listed spread execution differs from independently filled legs; reduced margin is not a loss cap.

Existing commodity paired-data acquisition gates remain unresolved; this paper brings no new input dataset or
executable rule. Its citation to Collins2007 explicitly notes the roll anticipation trade was not always profitable.
No numerical edge, flow dollar size, effective event count or cost threshold is invented.
