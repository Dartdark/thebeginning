## 1. What is it about?

A qualitative business-strategy case study: how Nasdaq tried to expand into Japan and Europe, subsequently acquired the established
OMX network, and experimented with Bitcoin-related exchange services and blockchain recordkeeping. The main contrast is between
building interdependent new markets/technology and using established capabilities with bounded experiments.

The “theoretical models” are participant/transaction flowcharts and conceptual management propositions, not asset-pricing,
forecasting, stochastic-control or algorithmic-trading models. Bitcoin appears as a technology/business ecosystem, not a tested
asset-return signal. Corporate strategy in this article means exchange-business decisions, not our portfolio trading rules.

## 2. What does it find?

The authors interpret the earlier Japan/Europe failures through market-cycle timing, partner capabilities, conflicts and technology
delays, and view the established OMX acquisition and later smaller fintech ventures more favorably. Their four propositions are:

| Proposition | Source's claim |
|---|---|
|1 | Replicating success depends on transferring routines and tacit knowledge to the new market |
|2 | Information asymmetry impedes efficient functioning of market participants |
|3 | Combining market-manager and market-producer roles creates conflicting incentives |
|4 | Depending on a resource not yet created/implemented does not establish competitive advantage |

The source expressly states it has **no evidence of collusion** in discussing the conflict proposition. Do not turn an incentive
analysis into an allegation of observed misconduct. The authors acknowledge a small sample of initiatives and recommend a broader
cross-sectional study. They do not estimate causal effects or test these propositions statistically.

The blockchain section describes Private Market recordkeeping using Open Assets/Chain, X-Stream licensing to Noble Markets and
a Swedish Bitcoin ETN listing (pp.92–93). These are distinct historical initiatives, not evidence that a single blockchain system
ran all Nasdaq trading, proved current settlement performance or predicted Bitcoin/NQ prices.

The abstract/conclusion claim drastically reduced transaction/recordkeeping costs, but the article does not supply a controlled
before/after cost dataset, measured latency distribution, fill-quality comparison, costed deployment ledger or quantified causal
estimate supporting that magnitude. An announced pilot or cited business report must not be relabeled verified execution savings.
No trading return, Sharpe, drawdown, forecast accuracy or portfolio backtest is reported.
