# Obizhaeva–Wang: retain for execution research; no directional strategy test

Summary: This is a large-order execution model, not an empirically tested alpha signal.
Liquidity replenishment changes the optimal schedule: blocks at the endpoints, flow between.
Selected model calculations reproduce the paper's 4.32%–11.92% impact-cost savings example.
Those percentages are not portfolio returns, total-fee savings or measured NQ/MNQ execution results.
Retain the execution lesson; do not change entries, exits, size or the trading plan.

## 1. What is it about?

How should an investor execute an already-decided, large buy order before a deadline?
The model minimizes its expected execution cost. The baseline trader is risk neutral;
Section 8.3 adds risk aversion. Order direction and total quantity are given, rather
than predicted by the model (pp. 3–4).

The book has constant density q above the ask. Trading produces a permanent price
shift lambda times quantity and a transient component that decays exponentially
at rate rho. Fundamental value follows Brownian motion. These are modeling
assumptions; the authors explicitly describe the book dynamics as given without
an equilibrium justification (pp. 5–9, 28).

## 2. What does it find?

For the risk-neutral, constant-parameter model, Proposition 3 gives:

- Initial block: X / (rho T + 2).
- Continuous middle: trade at rate rho X / (rho T + 2).
- Final block: X / (rho T + 2).

The middle's **total** quantity is rho T X / (rho T + 2). Faster replenishment
or more time shifts more quantity into the middle (pp. 18–20). Risk aversion
front-loads execution; the simple schedule is not universally optimal once the
model assumptions change (pp. 26–27).

At rho T = 2, the schedule is 25% initially, 50% during the interval and 25% at
the end. Section 7.2/Table 2 compares this with uniform-rate execution:

| Model case | Reduction in modeled price-impact cost |
|---|---:|
| lambda = 1/(2q) | 4.32% |
| lambda = 0 | 11.92% |

The committed arithmetic script reproduces both numbers to printed precision.
The illustration uses 100,000 shares, q=5,000 and a one-day horizon; it is not a
calibration to our futures orders. The denominator excludes the initial ask-value
term, including its spread component. Brokerage commissions and our native micro
fee assumptions are not part of this cost comparison (pp. 21–23).

There is **no new empirical profitable-trading test** in this draft. Section 7.3
suggests that institutional schedules could help explain U-shaped intraday volume
and spread patterns, then explicitly leaves empirical tests for future research
(p. 24). The theoretical replenishment rate is not an estimated NQ constant.

## 3. Why does it make money, if it does?

It potentially saves execution cost on a required trade: consuming liquidity
changes what is available next, so a schedule can exploit replenishment while
meeting a completion deadline. It does not establish that we can earn a positive
return by predicting someone else's order or fading a recent price move.

The model's hypothetical institution knows its own parent order. We do not observe
that parent's signed remaining quantity, start/deadline or whether an observed
price displacement is temporary impact rather than news. Inferring a forced buy
from a rising price would insert a different, untested momentum/reversal rule.
The paper supplies no ex-ante signed NQ/ES demand series or forecast-profit estimate.

## 4. Can we build on it?

**Yes as an execution reference; no immediate standalone strategy on the evidence supplied.**
Related library entries are E07 (slicing versus price risk), E05–E06 (missed fills
and delay opportunity cost), and E25 (existing NQ order-flow evidence). Those are
related topics, not an independent replication of this paper.

The inspected owned `data/OF_1min_2026_1.csv` includes one-minute spread, top bid/ask
size, imbalance, depletion and other summaries. Their presence does not supply
event-sequenced depth updates, exact shock/receipt times, queue state or identifiable
parent orders. One-minute price bars cannot by themselves identify the assumed
exponential impact decay separately from fundamental repricing and subsequent flow.
A bounded filename search under data/live did not locate a separately named raw
depth/quote/MBO/MBP dataset; this is not a claim about every possible external holding.

Our 1–20 micro orders may have little self-impact, but that must be measured, not
assumed. Slicing can delay a short-lived signal; one contract cannot be divided
into smaller same-contract child orders. A theoretical institutional schedule does
not justify delaying a strategy's protective stop or changing its position size.

The smallest useful next check, when an appropriate recorded dataset is available,
is an observation-only comparison of actual parent size with contemporaneous
spread/depth and realized shortfall on the same native contract. Establish a
material self-impact problem first. Only then preregister a fixed-parent-order
execution comparison with point-in-time depth/replenishment estimates, integer
child quantities, latency, adverse selection, unfilled quantity and opportunity
cost. Fit parameters on earlier observations and keep a later evaluation period.
No logger installation, subscription, broker/VPS action or new data capture was performed.

## Decision and limits

**Do not backtest a directional trading strategy from this paper.** The source does
not define an observable, pre-trade signed flow signal for our contracts, and an
execution study lacks the event-level inputs needed to test its central mechanism.
Record it as E39 in the execution library, rather than ranking an untested strategy
in TIER_LIST or placing an execution-only idea in the promising-strategy queue.

The arithmetic check verifies selected formulas, not the full optimal-control
proof, empirical robustness, native fills or actual savings. No Sharpe, funded
payout or other strategy-performance metric is warranted from this source review.
