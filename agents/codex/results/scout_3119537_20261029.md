## What is it about?
How order imbalance, matched quantity and indicative auction prices evolve before U.S. equity auctions.

## What does it find?
The auction-update sample runs September28 2016–January12 2018. Section2 discloses a roughly four-update/second
provider cap that can miss liquid-SPY events. Sections4.2–4.4 report indicative-price mean reversion, changing
responses by venue/auction and asset, and movement toward the continuous-book midpoint. These are descriptive
properties of an auction state, not fills or a net trading backtest.

## Why does it make money, if it does?
No profitable executable strategy is demonstrated here. Benchmark-constrained demand can invite offsetting
liquidity, but predicting an indicative price is different from buying or selling at it. Publication, order
submission and auction eligibility intervene. Cancellations and reference-price changes can mimic new demand.

## Can we build on it?
Potentially: refine the existing real-imbalance SPY/ES or QQQ/NQ hypothesis using persistence and contemporaneous
quote controls. This is not a new independent family. Source auction rules are historical, not current authority.
The timing diagrams and provider cap argue against copying an event-by-event response function into a bar test.
The HTML equations also appear to have midpoint/sign/index notation problems: use exchange definitions and
validate any formulas before implementation, rather than copying rendered symbols.
