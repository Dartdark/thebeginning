                                         Abstract
   Traditional dip-buying strategies rely heavily on indicators like Relative Strength Index
(RSI), Bollinger Bands, and moving averages to time entries, but they fail to measure the
actual velocity and strength of the subsequent bullish pullbacks. To bridge this gap, we
introduce in this study a novel, physics-inspired technical indicator: the Coefficient of
Restitution (COR) indicator. Adapting the physics principles of collision dynamics –
where the coefficient of restitution measures kinetic energy retention after impact – the
COR indicator is mathematically defined as the ratio of the absolute differences between
asset and market RSI values before and after price dips, offering a quantitative gauge of
pullback strength relative to broader market conditions. Using a rigorous backtesting
framework with walk-forward optimization across multiple timeframes (from 30-minutes
to daily intervals), we evaluated the COR strategy on Magnificent Seven stocks and
leveraged ETFs, incorporating volatility-adjusted position sizing via Average True Range
(ATR). Our findings show that the COR strategy significantly outperforms traditional
mean-reversion frameworks, particularly on high-volatility assets. Notably, at the 1-hour
timeframe, the COR indicator combined with the ATR sizing achieved 41.56% total
return, an 85.71% win rate, and a Sharpe Ratio of 1.51. These results provide market
practitioners with a robust quantitative framework to validate rebound strength and assets
trade quality prior to execution.

1. Introduction
Swing trading constitutes a vital segment of quantitative asset management, capturing
short-to-medium-term market inefficiencies by holding positions across horizons of
several days to weeks. Within this paradigm, mean-reversion and structural dip-buying
strategies have gained popularity, systematically exploiting temporary price depreciations
at established support levels within broader macroeconomic uptrends. However,
practitioners tend to struggle to timing appropriate entry spots and resulting in severe
drawdowns or capital stagnation during periods of extended market corrections.
Achieving enhanced risk-adjusted returns requires a mathematically sound entry timing
approach derived through robust, multi-dimensional validation metrics.

Conventionally, systematic swing traders rely on a deterministic composite of classic
technical indicators – principally the Relative Strength Index (RSI), Bollinger Bands, and
Moving Averages – to map structural entry points. While this traditional baseline strategy
can identify oversold conditions, it possesses a fundamental architectural limitation: it
fails to quantify strength and velocity of the subsequent bullish pullback. This blind spot
arises because conventional metrics evaluate asset momentum in isolation based entirely
on historical, idiosyncratic price series. Consequently, they ignore broader market-wide
dynamics and systematic risk factors. Such a structural omission prompts a critical
quantitative question: When an entry signal is triggered by conventional mean-reversion
parameters, how can practitioners reliably quantify the underlying momentum of the
bullish pullback to ensure it possesses sufficient kinetic energy to breach immediate
resistance zones?

To handle this limitation, this paper introduces the Coefficient of Restitution (COR)
strategy, based on a novel, physics-inspired technical indicator. The conceptual framework
of the COR indicator is mathematically derived from Newtonian collision mechanics –
specifically the classic bouncing ball problem [1] – where the coefficient of restitution
measures the conservation of kinetic energy and velocity retention post-impact. In
physical systems, this coefficient dictates the maximum rebound height achievable after a
collision. Translating this principle into financial econometrics, the COR indicator
operates as an advanced filtering layer alongside traditional signal generators. While
conventional indicators establish the preliminary trade architecture, the COR indicator
dynamically assesses position quality by validating the systemic strength of the price
rebound relative to broader market momentum.

Consequently, the COR framework provides traders with an ex-ante risk management tool
to verify trade viability before capital allocation. The remainder of this study is organized
as follows: Section 2 delineates the underlying theoretical framework; Section 3 details
the empirical methodology and walk-forward optimization framework; Section 4 presents
the backtesting results and performance metrics across the Magnificent Seven and
leveraged ETFs; and Section 5 discusses the implications of the findings for algorithmic
execution.

Our work targets Project Track 7 (Medium/Long‑Term Trading Models), Topic 1 (Swing

2.2.1 Volatility Measures & Performance
A central result involving scaling exposure inversely to recently realized volatility (among
volatility‑managed portfolios) is that it increases Sharpe ratios and generates large alphas
across market and factor strategies because changes in volatility are not matched by
proportional changes in expected returns [6]. In particular, related work showed that
momentum and similar trend strategies suffer crash risk in high‑volatility episodes, which
inspired volatility‑targeting or scaling as a risk‑management overlay [7], which is an idea
closely aligned with our project’s volatility‑aware sizing and exits. At the same time,
out‑of‑sample and market‑specific evidence indicates that the efficacy of volatility
management can attenuate or fail in certain contexts, underscoring the importance of
sensitivity estimation, regime segmentation, and external validity checks for dip‑trip
implementations.

Our proposed use of Average True Range (ATR) for price movement volatility assessment
[8] as a price‑scaled range‑based volatility proxy for entry conditioning and stop and
target calibration is standard in practice and consistent with our project’s algorithmic
blueprint, and references to dip‑search strategies using volatility bands. Realized volatility
computed from high‑frequency or daily returns provides a complementary measure shown
to relate to cross‑sectional momentum dynamics at the industry level, supporting the
dual‑proxy approach for sensitivity analysis at trade entry.

2.4. Competitor Analysis
While we have not found any paper that exactly describes modeling trading signals after
the coefficient of restitution for the dip trip strategy, there are related works which
modelled after collision dynamics and elastic systems, and from volatility measures for
trading signals.

The first competitor stems from Volatility‑Managed Portfolios [6], where the authors
improve Sharpe ratios and alphas across factors by reducing exposure when volatility is
high. However, the performance can attenuate out‑of‑sample or differ across markets, thus
requiring careful validation [7]. In our project, we aim to develop a principled template
for volatility‑aware sizing overlay on dip‑trip entries.

Next, Bergfast introduced a dip-searching swing strategy [8] which directly targets dips
with band and range‑based logic aligned to our project’s entry design framework.
However, it is based on a simpler rule set without modern walk‑forward and sensitivity
quantification. Our proposed framework thus serves as a baseline to benchmark
incremental value of volatility‑sensitive sizing and exits.

Also from volatility, we have Volatility‑Scaled Momentum [12], where volatility targeting
reduces crash risk and improves risk‑adjusted returns in trend strategies. However,
factor‑level results may not translate one‑for‑one to single‑name dip entries. In contrast,
our project offers a playbook for scaling exposure and risk during high‑vol states in the
dip‑trip strategy.

On the side of physics-inspired approaches, there was work on modeling price action with
velocity and mass concepts stemming from the definition of momentum in physics [10].
The paper established relationships of prices as mass particles but in a financial context,
and related it to physical momentum but in terms of price action. However, the
relationships were not adopted to trading strategies such as the dip trip and remain to be
evaluated and enhanced.

In summary, our project is aimed at exploiting buying dip opportunities through
“volatility-sensitive sizing and exits” approach adopted from natural occurring events like
dropping and bouncing ball, combined with a “walk-forward backtesting” which sets its
uniqueness among competing research works, specially by introducing the missing energy
retention gauge for pullbacks.

2.5. Backtesting Guardrails
The plan to use walk‑forward optimization with fixed parameters during testing directly
addresses data‑snooping concerns raised in classical critiques of technical trading studies,
where after rule selection the results often overstate efficacy. Incorporating statistical
guardrails inspired by the data‑snooping literature and maintaining minimum win rate and
per‑trade return constraints further reduces the risk of spurious rule discovery and
supports credible slope estimation for volatility and profitability sensitivity analysis.

2.6. Transaction Costs Considerations
Trading cost realism materially affects short‑horizon strategies, and large‑scale
institutional evidence indicates that, when measured accurately with live trading data,
capacity and net returns of anomalies such as momentum can be substantially larger than
once thought, while short‑term reversal styles are more constrained by costs [9]. This
underscores the importance of modeling slippage and impact explicitly in the backtest and
of favoring holding periods and entry and exit mechanics that preserve edge net of costs
for swing‑horizon dip‑trip trades.

2.7. The Data Considerations
Furthermore, our decision to test across US equities and potentially add diversified asset
classes is appropriate for assessing sensitivity stability, with the caveat that volatility
management effects can vary across markets, suggesting regime identification or
stratification by volatility state to improve external validity of the estimated slope [7].
Explicit regime tagging during walk‑forward validation (e.g., high‑volatility versus
low‑volatility windows) will thus help determine when volatility increases are beneficial
or detrimental for dip‑trip profitability.

3.2. The Traditional Buying Dip Approach
The traditional buying dip approach is employed as a benchmark to evaluate the
performance of the COR buying dip strategy that we uniquely develop in this project.
Such an approach is deemed to be traditional as it is one of the commonly used by the
buying dip practitioners and it consists of a simultaneous use of Relative Strength Index
(RSI), Bollinger Bands, and Moving Averages to time their entries in bullish pullbacks of
the asset prices.

From the perspective of the benchmark or traditional strategy described above, the buying
dip signal will be triggered by a combination of the following circumstances of the asset
price that is moving upward after bouncing on the support level:

    ●​ The current asset price is above the long-term Simple Moving Average;

    ●​ The current asset price is below the 30-level of the RSI indicator, meaning the
       asset is still undervalued; and

    ●​ The current asset price is crossing the lower band of the Bollinger Bands
       indicator, confirming the asset undervaluation.

3.3. The COR Strategy
The COR buying dip strategy, as developed in this work, combines the traditional buying
dip approach and the COR indicator. While the traditional strategy will simply tell the
trader what the optimal entry spot or price is, the COR indicator provides information on
the strength of the bullish pullback.

Inspired by the Physics Coefficient of Restitution where it indicates the amount of kinetic
energy remaining in the bouncing ball right after the collision, as introduced in a prior
section above, when the RSI of the asset dips significantly and depending on market
conditions via the market’s RSI, 𝑒 tells us how much propensity there is for the asset price
to rebound. For example, if the market’s RSI before and after remain about the same
while a large dip was observed for the asset’s RSI, then the asset’s price is expected to
rebound back to a level aligned to the market’s RSI.

As for the market component of the RSI in the formula for COR, we use the S&P 500
index (Ticker: ^GSPC) as an indicator of the broader market. Both asset and market data
would have the same frequency and timeframe.

In the implementation of the COR strategy the buy signal is cumulatively triggered by all
conditions described in the traditional strategy plus a COR value not below a preset
threshold. The COR lower threshold is set in order to make sure that at the entry price the
asset has enough likelihood to continue in the profitable bullish movement.

As part of the risk management procedure, the algorithm uses the volatility-adjusted
position sizing. Volatility adjustment of the position size involves the use of Average True
Range as discussed in the Theoretical Framework. The risk (proportion of portfolio cash)
was also included as a hyperparameter for optimization, ranging between 10% and 90%.

3.4. Exit signal generation
Both strategies we consider in this project rely on the same exit approach. The algorithm
will trigger the exit signal as one of the following conditions is met:

    ●​ The current asset price hits the predefined take profit level;

    ●​ The current asset price hits the predefined stop loss level; or

    ●​ The asset movement is experiencing mean reversion.

For the purpose of the goals and objectives of this research the take profit level will be set
at a predefined threshold not below the halfway to the nearest resistance level from the
support zone. On the other hand, the stop loss is configured as a trailing stop loss which
looks to exit the position close to the reversion price.

3.5. Local backtesting
The plan to use a single-step, out-of-sample chronological split to preserve data integrity
and prevent lookahead bias with fixed parameters during testing directly addresses
data‑snooping concerns raised in classical critiques of technical trading studies, where
after rule selection the results often overstate efficacy. This strict chronological train-test
split was thus implemented rather than traditional randomized cross-validation. In
particular, for each asset, the 1-year historical dataset spanning October 10, 2024, to
October 10, 2025, was segmented into two distinct, non-overlapping windows using an
optimization split factor of 0.70. The training data spanned approximately October 2024
to late June 2025. This data was utilized to execute an exhaustive grid search across all
parameter combinations defined in the optimization grid. The parameter set that
maximizes the designated objective function “Total Return” was selected for use in the
test dataset, which spanned approximately late June 2025 to October 10, 2025. Note that
the optimal parameter set discovered in the training window was locked and evaluated
exactly once on this unseen test data to compile the final performance metrics (Total
Return, Win Rate, Sharpe Ratio, and Maximum Drawdown) reported in Section 4.

For parameters that form the trading signal and the underlying derivations that lead to
specific conditions of the signal, they are added to the optimization grid, each with a
range of values for hyperparameter search. As there is a large number of combinations,
optimization is performed in parallel across all available CPUs locally.

different timeframes.

5. Discussion
From our initial optimization results with a stock ticker from the magnificent seven
companies in the US, it was found that there was no clear relationship between higher
intervals of asset prices and a higher total return and win rate. Moreover, with ATR for
position sizing in trades, the total returns, win rates and Sharpe ratios were found to be
higher for the same asset and frequency. These results thus indicate the importance of
ATR in risk adjustments to our trades. Our results also showed that having COR in the
strategy helped increase the win-rate and Sharpe ratio without sacrificing much returns,
which indicated that our proposed COR strategy is effective in ‘securing’ wins when
added to the vanilla strategy involving the BB and RSI. Moreover, there are a notably
small number of trades during the evaluation period, which places results with high (or
perfect) win-rates with caution due to the low number of trades to evaluate the
effectiveness of our strategy.
We have also established a relationship with higher volatility assets (e.g. Tesla stock)
having greater potential for returns, especially with ATR. This volatility relationship with
returns was found to be exacerbated with leverage, which was validated with the
leveraged ETF for Tesla (Ticker: TSLL), with and without ATR. As currently we have
only evaluated a stock’s performance with a leveraged version of its asset, to further
extend our hypothesis and results, we would thus expand the comparisons in future to
more pairs of assets and their leveraged versions. This would help to further investigate
potential relationships between volatility of assets with their total returns, with and
without ATR. Combinations of metrics will likewise also be sequentially evaluated
starting with our proposed COR metric, adding in more like Bollinger Bands, RSI, leading
to a combination of all metrics to determine the trading signal.

6. Conclusion
Our research successfully demonstrated the potential of physics-inspired technical
analysis through the development and implementation of the Coefficient of Restitution
(COR) indicator for swing trading applications. The proposed COR strategy addresses a
fundamental limitation in traditional dip-buying approaches by providing quantitative
assessment of pullback strength, thereby improving entry timing and trade quality
evaluation. The primary contribution of this work resides in the novel adaptation of
collision dynamics principles to financial market analysis. By translating the physics
concept of energy retention after collision (i.e. COR) into a metric for measuring price
momentum recovery, the COR indicator fills a critical gap in existing technical analysis
frameworks. The mathematical formulation in (12) successfully captures the relationship
between asset-specific price movements and broader market conditions, providing traders
with actionable insights about potential trade outcomes.
Furthermore, our empirical analysis reveals several important insights. Firstly, the
integration of ATR-based position sizing significantly enhances performance metrics
across all timeframes, demonstrating the value of volatility-aware risk management.
Secondly, more volatile assets with leverage consistently produced superior returns and
risk-adjusted performance, with the 1-hour interval achieving exceptional results
including 85.71% win rates and positive Sharpe ratios. In particular, with ATR, the 1-hour
interval yielded the highest total return of 41.56% with COR and 43.77% without COR,
and a high Sharpe ratio of 1.51 with COR and 1.35 without COR. Lastly, the COR
strategy's performance improvements over traditional methods validate the theoretical
foundation and practical applicability of physics-inspired indicators in financial markets.
Overall, while our initial results appear promising, there are several limitations which
warrant acknowledgment. The current analysis focuses primarily on selected stocks from
the magnificent seven in the US and selected leveraged ETFs, which are large companies
and funds with highly liquid assets during specific market conditions, potentially limiting



18
generalizability. As a next step, we seek to expand the universe of assets to include
diverse sectors, asset classes, historical volatilities and market regimes to validate the
COR indicator's robustness across varying conditions. More historical data would also be
extracted for each frequency, subject to the limits of Yahoo Finance’s API, which in our
frequencies contexts are 60 days for 30-minute data, 2 years for hourly data, and years
(non-stated limit) for daily data. Moreover, our study period's predominantly bullish
market conditions may have favored dip-buying strategies. Additional testing during
known bear markets and high-volatility regimes will be crucial for comprehensive
strategy validation. The integration of regime identification algorithms could further
enhance the COR strategy's adaptability to changing market conditions.

hyperparameter search and isolate the mathematical edge of the technical signals, the
backtest operated under the assumptions that transaction costs, execution commissions,
slippage, and risk-free rates were zero.
