          The Buffett Indicator: International Evidence∗

                      Laurens Swinkels†                Thomas S. Umlauft‡

                                          30 March 2022



                                               Abstract

           Warren Buffett suggested that the ratio of the market value of publicly traded
       stocks to economic output could identify equity market mispricings. We extend the
       existing research from the United States to international equity markets by investigat-
       ing the return-predictive characteristics of the market value of equity-to-gross domestic
       product for a dataset comprising fourteen countries. The findings corroborate that the
       “Buffet indicator” explains a large fraction of ten-year return variation for the major-
       ity of countries outside the United States. Low ratios have predicted above-average
       investment returns, while periods of high ratios have, on average, been followed by
       below-average returns over the subsequent ten-year period. We also compare the “Buf-
       fett indicator” to other well-known stock market valuation signals and phenomena, such
       as the cyclically-adjusted price-to-earnings ratio and mean-reversion in stock returns.




Introduction
At the turn of the millennium, Buffett wondered about the exceptional performance as
measured by the Dow Jones Industrial Average during the 17-year window between 1981 and
1998 compared to the dismal performance during the equally long period between 1964 and
1981 in spite of a significantly more beneficial macroeconomic setup for the latter period, as
economic output increased at more than twice the rate between 1964 and 1981 than between
1981-1998. In two Forbes interviews (Buffett and Loomis 1999, 2001), Buffett suggested
that the market value of equity (MVE) scaled by gross domestic product (GDP), which
subsequently will be referred to as the MVE/GDP ratio, would have heralded higher returns

for 1981-1998 by indicating cheaper valuations for the stock market. In spite of Buffett’s
claim that the ratio of market capitalisation to economic output is “probably the best single
measure of where valuations stand at any given moment”, its forecast ability has drawn
comparatively little academic scrutiny.1 Our research aims to fill this gap.
The intuition behind the MVE/GDP ratio as a valuation signal is simple. When the price of
equities, as a claim on future profits, goes up without a commensurate increase in economic
output, the forward earnings yield decreases, rendering, ceteris paribus, equity investments
less attractive. Vis-à-vis solely price-oriented valuation techniques, such as the cyclically-
adjusted price-to-earnings ratio, MVE/GDP has the advantage that its nominator contains
information on quality (price) as well as quantities (equity issuance) and thereby incorpo-
rating investor sentiment not captured by purely price-based valuation metrics. New share
issuance increases (decreases) when equity markets are overvalued (undervalued) if corporate
managers are able to time the market; see Baker and Wurgler (2002).
The main challenge with using the MVE/GDP ratio as a signal for long-horizon market
timing purposes is the lack of a theoretical fundamental level for the ratio to converge to in
equilibrium. This is one of the reasons why at each point in time, there exist substantial
differences for the MVE/GDP ratio across countries. For example, by year-end 2019, the
ratio stood at 148% in the U.S. and only 55% in Germany. The financial structure and
the regulatory environment are important factors determining the size of the stock market
in a country. More specifically, the stock market of bank-based systems may reasonably
be expected to be of less importance than in market-based systems, rendering a direct
comparison between countries difficult. Rajan and Zingales (2003) also point out that not
only is the variance of economies’ equilibrium fraction of stock market capitalisation to GDP
pronounced, but that there exist substantial cross-sectional differences,2 cautioning against
blindly extrapolating the findings of any country to other countries. This is one of the
reasons why we look in first instance at time-series predictability of this valuation ratio and
not at cross-sectional predictability from the ratio itself. However, the predicted returns
from the time-series analysis will be used to determine which countries have an attractively
valued stock market, and which countries have not. Hence, the trading strategy invests in
countries for which the MVE/GDP ratio is low compared to its own history, which is not the
same as an investment into a country with low MVE/GDP ratio compared to other countries.
This direct cross-sectional comparison is not useful if fair value levels of the signal across
countries are different. But even within countries, there may not necessarily exist a time-
robust equilibrium value. The fair value level may change over time, as the total value of
the stock market is the discounted value of future cash-flows derived from share ownership.
The market value therefore increases when cash-flows are expected to have higher growth
than previously predicted, or if these cash flows are discounted at a lower discount rate.

The discount rate used to calculate the present value of a company’s dividend stream may
be affected by the long-term interest rate. Equity markets may appear expensive (cheap)
when long-term interest rates are low (high). Expecting mean-reversion in the MVE/GDP
ratio may therefore be partially related to an expectation of mean-reversion in long-term
interest rates. Also, the willingness to be a public firm may change over time (see, Doidge,
Karolyi, and Stulz (2017) and McDonald IV (2022)). Moreover, if listed companies transform
to multinational companies or a stock exchange attracts foreign companies, the link with
domestic economic growth weakens. Although these trends tend to be slow moving processes,
they may create noise to this valuation signal. Each of these challenges make it worthwhile
to examine whether the “Buffett indicator” can be used for long-horizon market timing.
The contribution of this paper is threefold. First, we analyse the international evidence of
the forecasting ability of the MVE/GDP ratio for equity market returns. Our research com-
plements existing research that is limited to the U.S. (see Lleo and Ziemba 2018 and Umlauft
2020). Our data goes back to 1973, therefore substantially extending the international study
by Chang and Pak (2018) that starts in 1997.3 Second, we compare this predictor to other
well-known stock market valuation signals, such as the cyclically-adjusted price-to-earnings
ratio (CAPE) of Campbell and Shiller (1988, 1998, 2001) and mean-reversion in stock returns
as documented by, e.g., Poterba and Summers (1987), Fama and French (1988), Spierdijk,
Bikker and Van den Hoek (2012), Moskowitz, Ooi and Pedersen (2012) and Zaremba, Kizys,
and Raza (2020). Third, we provide empirical evidence for real-time profitability over the
backtest period 1985-2019, supplementing the existing literature on long-run stock return
predictability (see, e.g., Goyal and Welch (2008) and Diris, Palm, and Schotman (2015)).
Our findings can be summarised as follows: First, using data from the stock markets of
fourteen countries with data going back to 1973, we show that the “Buffett indicator” pos-
sesses exceptional forecast qualities over longer horizons. More specifically, we find that
MVE/GDP explains 83% of overall return variation, ranging from as low as 42% for Austria
to 94% for Great Britain. Using an expanding window instead of a the full sample reduces
the explanatory power to still considerable 64%. Secondly, in a predictive regression where
we include MVE/GDP, naive mean-reversion in returns, and the cyclically-adjusted price
earnings ratio, only MVE/GDP is statistically significant, suggesting that our findings are
not subsumed by other popular valuation signals. Importantly, these research findings may
be transported to practical application. Our backtest shows that a simple strategy based
on investing in countries with the highest model-predicted returns would have yielded sta-
tistically significant and economically meaningful alpha over a corresponding buy-and-hold

The remainder of this article is structured as follows: Section 2 explains the data used for the
analysis and outlines the methodology. Section 3 presents the empirical results and Section
4 performs a robustness test. In Section 5, the empirical results are discussed. Section 6
summarises the article and provides a conclusion.


Data and methodology
The countries covered in this article include OECD countries with quarterly data available
for market capitalization and gross domestic product starting in 1973. The year 1973 is a
natural cut-off date since data for the majority of countries starts in that year. In addition,
the requirement of ten-year forward returns in order to evaluate the forecast quality means
that we have only 38 years even with data going back to 1973. The 1973 cut-off excludes
Norway, New Zealand, Luxemburg, Portugal, Greece, Finland, Sweden and Spain. Exclusion
thus mainly affects smaller countries, the only exemption being Spain, whose data starts
only in 1987. Our final sample consists of fourteen countries. Importantly, all G7 nations –
Canada, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, United Kingdom, and the United States – make the
cut. Smaller countries with sufficient data include Australia, Austria, Belgium, Denmark,
Ireland, the Netherlands as well as Switzerland.
For the construction of the market value of equity-to-gross domestic product (MVE/GPD)
ratio, the following data series were used: The nominator, or market value of equity, is
based on Refinitiv’s Thomson Reuters Datastream country index data, respectively, the
total market series (TOTMK. . . ) in local currencies. The denominator for the ratio is based
on quarterly nominal gross domestic product (annualised) data sourced from the OECD
Quarterly National Accounts statistics: Gross domestic product - expenditure approach –
National currency, current prices, annual levels, seasonally adjusted (series code: B1_GE
measure: CARSA).4 Data is in local currencies.

compute MVE/GDP figures starting from the same date in the context of comparative
model evaluation in section 4. In other words, 1983 constitutes the default start date for
both P/E10 as well as MVE/GDP in section 4.1. For Denmark, France and Italy, whose data

periods accordingly as well. Returns are derived from Datastream Global Index (DSGI)
data, using the total return index (RI) function of the total market (TOTMK. . . ) series.
The returns are denominated in local currencies and always include dividends.
Short (three-month) rates for computing excess returns are sourced from Global Financial
Data (GFD). Missing data was complemented by three-month money market rates provided
by the OECD for Austria from January 1991 onwards and from January 2009 onwards

beginning of the period. For Switzerland, the ten-year government bond rate was used
between March 1973 and December 1979, instead. For Denmark, the policy rate of the
central bank of Denmark was used for the period between March 1973 and December 1975.

Indeed, the presence of overlapping time-periods introduces subtle but severe statistical
problems. The common solution to this problem has traditionally been to calculate
autocorrelation-robust standard errors based on Hansen and Hodrick (1980) and Newey
and West (1987). As the serial correlation induced in the error terms by overlapping
data is often very strong, however, the obtained robust estimators tend to have rather
poor properties for finite samples. Thus, commonly used methods for autocorrelation
correction may be inadequate and misleading in finite sample data.7 Fortunately, more
recent theoretical and practical analyses have come up with better procedures to correct
for the bias induced by overlapping forecast horizons. In addition to the commonly used
Newey and West (1987) heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent (HAC) standard
errors, we adjust for the overlap-induced bias by computing adjusted t-statistics following
Hjalmarsson (2008, 2011) who demonstrates that, instead of using autocorrelation-robust
standard errors, a parsimonious technique of dividing the t-statistics by the square root of
the forecasting horizon yields not only approximate t-statistics but produces asymptotically
exact corrections.

Empirical results
We start with charting the evolution of MVE/GDP. For practical purposes, we show an equal-
weighted aggregate of the fourteen-country sample (AGG) along with the United States as
a yardstick. The Appendix contains figures for all individual countries. As Figure 2 makes
clear, MVE/GDP is highly variable over time. It is low in the beginning of our sample, when
financial development through the stock market was less common for many of the countries
in our international sample, and increases dramatically towards the end of the sample period.
Moreover, we see suspicious peaks around market tops, especially since the middle of the
1990s. In the remainder of the article, we evaluate whether MVE/GDP is a predictor for
equity returns for our sample of international equity markets, complementing the work of
Umlauft (2020) on the United States.
Figure 3 depicts graphically the relationship between MVE/GDP and subsequent returns
for the equal-weighted sample and the United States, demonstrating the close inverse rela-
tionship between starting valuations and ten-year forward returns.
Another way to look at this is to examine the model-based expected stock market returns
with the realised stock market returns over the subsequent ten-year period. Figure 4 dis-
plays observed trailing ten-year returns along with ten-year lagged model-predicted ten-year
forward returns for the equal-weighted country aggregate and the United States. The chart
illustrates that the simple model captures a significant degree of return variation, explaining
approximately 83% for the period between 1973 and 2019.

Relation to other popular valuation signals
By design, the MVE/GDP ratio is constructed with a fast-changing nominator and a per-
sistent denominator. The dominance of MVE in the MVE/GDP ratio gives rise to the
possibility that the forward return estimate obtained from regressing on the MVE/GDP
ratio may be driven solely by the nominator. Abstracting from new share issuance and buy-
backs, the increases and decreases in MVE are caused by realised stock returns. Hence, the
predictive ability of the “Buffett indicator” may simply be due to mean-reversion in stock
returns, i.e. high past returns are followed by low future returns, and vice versa. We inves-
tigate the relation with mean-reversion in stock returns in the next subsection. In addition,
one can wonder whether the use of GDP is the most logical fundamental for comparison, as
it includes economic activity that is not captured by the market for listed equity. Another
popular equity market valuation measure looks at the market value of equities relative to
the earnings that these companies have produced over the past ten years. In the second
subsection, we therefore compare the predictive ability of the “Buffett indicator” relative to
the cyclically-adjusted price-earnings ratio.


Mean-reversion in equity market returns
Given the negative correlation, MVE/GDP hence may capture a mean-reversion effect, as
observed by e.g., Poterba and Summers (1987), Fama and French (1988), Spierdijk, Bikker
and Van den Hoek (2012) and Zaremba, Kizys, and Raza (2020). To assess whether the
MVE/GDP forecast quality is driven by (naïve) mean-reversion, we regress ten-year forward
returns on five- and ten-year past returns. Table 5 below depicts R2 values for ten-year
forward returns with two different lookback windows. The lookback windows of five and ten
years are aligned to our forecast horizon and show that, at least a one-size-fits-all, mean-
reversion approach has limited explanatory power, explaining less than 10% and 1% of return
variation, on average, for the ten- and five-year lookback window, respectively.
In view of the low explanatory power of a naïve implementation of mean-reversion, we may
reject the possibility that the forecast quality of the market value of equity-to-gross domes-
tic product ratio may, to any substantial degree, be attributable to capturing an underlying
mean-reversion process. This is in-line with the mean-reversion literature. Spierdijk, Bikker
and Van den Hoek (2012), for instance, document mean-reversion half-lives ranging from 2.0
to 22.6 years with significant cross-sectional as well as time-series variability. We hence can-

Cyclically-adjusted price-earnings ratio
Over the past decades, the seminal articles of Campbell and Shiller (1988, 1998, 2001) have
rendered the so-called cyclically-adjusted price-earnings ratio (alternatively, CAPE, Shiller
P/E, P/E10) a de-facto benchmark for longer-term return forecasting. Hence, the following
section compares the forecast quality of MVE/GDP with that of the cyclically-adjusted price-
earnings ratio. Since P/E10 ratios require a ten-year formation period, data availability only
starts in 1983. In addition to the ten-year lag, data for Denmark, France and Italy are further
shortened due to later data availability (Denmark: 31 March 1988, France: 31 March 2000,
Italy: 31 March 1996). For comparability purposes, we compute MVE/GDP figures starting
from the same date. In other words, 1983 constitutes the default start date for both P/E10
as well as MVE/GDP in this section. For Denmark, France and Italy, whose data availability
for P/E10 data starts later, we adjusted MVE/GDP computation start dates accordingly.
Table 6 makes clear that compared to the cyclically-adjusted price-to-earnings ratio,
MVE/GDP is substantially more accurate for individual countries and for the pooled-
country aggregate, almost unexceptionally exhibiting materially higher t-values. Indeed,
P/E10 captures more of the return variation of only four countries: Denmark, France, Japan
and the United States. The difference for two countries – the United States (0.83 vs 0.80)
and Denmark (0.42 vs 0.41) – is minor, while P/E10’s R2 for France (0.83) is markedly
higher than that of the MVE/GDP ratio (0.66). For Japan, the MVE/GDP’s explanatory
power of 0.39 is also markedly lower than that the 0.47 for the cyclically-adjusted price-
to-earnings ratio. Interestingly, the United States is the only country apart from France
with a substantial R2 for P/E10 (0.83), although R2 for MVE/GDP is high as well (0.80).
Notably, the R2 of 0.77 for the equal-weighted pooled-country aggregate for MVE/GDP
compares quite favourably to the 0.27 for P/E10.

The ratio of market valuation of equity to economic output has a significant advantage
vis-à-vis solely price-oriented valuation techniques, such as the cyclically-adjusted price-to-
earnings ratio. Its nominator contains information on quality (price) as well as quantities
(equity issuance). Accounting for (net) equity issuances, for instance, incorporates investor
sentiment not captured by purely price-based valuation metrics. Research shows that firms
are able to exploit investor exuberance by timing the market and issuing relatively more
equities at overvalued prices around market tops, and thus before periods of low equity
returns (Loughran, Ritter, and Rydqvist 1994, Baker and Wurgler 2000). Also, more recently,
share buybacks have become a popular means to return money to shareholders (Grullon
and Michaely 2002). These share repurchases may be a signal by management that the
stock price is undervalued.8 MVE/GDP, thus, adds an additional information layer with

Practical application to investment strategies
In the following part, we transfer the empirical findings of this paper to a more practice-
oriented framework, by devising a few simple trading rules. Given the high explanatory
power of MVE/GDP in explaining subsequent returns, we test whether buying the countries
with the highest model-predicted returns would have outperformed a simple buy-and-hold
strategy. It should be noted that, unlike Lleo and Ziemba (2018), we are not concerned with
forecasting short-term price-action and drawdowns. We forecast long-term equity returns at
a ten-year horizon. Since, with very few exceptions, at a ten-year horizon equity returns are
strictly positive, timeseries strategies which attempt to time the market by varying equity
market exposure, i.e. deviate from full-allocation, are bound to underperform a buy-and-hold
benchmark unless leverage is employed. A more straight-forward way of utilising the time-
series return forecasts developed and tested in this paper is, therefore, the use of timeseries
forecasts for the purpose of allocating to the cross-section – in this context, countries – for
which the timeseries forecasts are most positive and excluding those with the least attractive
return predictions. It should also be noted that this is not a cross-sectional approach in the
traditional sense. In the introduction, we caution against comparing MVE/GDP ratios
across countries for a variety of reasons. We do not, however, allocate to countries with the
lowest MVE/GDP, as a traditional cross-sectional approach would. Rather, we use return
forecasts derived from timeseries analysis for the purpose of evaluating which countries are
expected to show the highest returns over subsequent ten-year time-period. A strategy based
on timeseries forecasts in the context of a cross-sectional approach holds the advantage of
making use of the extraordinarily accurate return forecasts permitted by MVE/GDP and at
the same time, the approach elegantly circumvents the practical problems associated with
forecasting long-term returns for asset classes with strictly positive risk premia.
The backtest is based on the following rules:

   1. We rank countries in ascending order on the basis of their model-predicted ten-year
      forward return. We then construct a portfolio which equal-weights the upper half
      of the fourteen-country dataset, i.e. invest in the seven countries with the highest
      model-predicted ten-year forward return. We ensure that portfolio construction uses
      only data which was known to investors at any given point in time by using an
      expanding window regression framework. Our dataset starts in March 1973, and since
      we perform regressions with a 10-year holding period, the first prediction is only
      available in June 1983. However, this prediction is based on a regression with only
      two (overlapping) observations. We allow the model an initial stabilisation period
      until year-end 1985, at which point 11 quarterly observations are available to make
ratio constitutes the “Excess CAPE Yield” measure which is defined as the CAPE yield above the ten-year

all ten tested strategies (see Table 8), which outperformed the benchmark by approximately
1%, on average. Remarkably, all portfolio characteristics (i.e. returns, volatility as well as
maximum drawdown) in each single year are better for the strategy than for the correspond-
ing benchmark. In fact, only during 129 out of the total of 3,550 months, or 3.6%, of all ten
backtested strategies, the strategy trailed the benchmark. Importantly, the trading strategy
is robust to the most conservative assumption regarding trading costs. Given that rebal-

portfolio. Since an average turnover of 20% would only be necessary in the unlikely scenario
that the full portfolio had to be sold and, analogously, a new portfolio had to be created
in full (i.e. there is zero overlap between old and new portfolio), the assumption meets the
criterium of cautious conservativism. Assuming high transaction costs of 1%, the trading
strategy would have incurred costs of only 20 basis points p.a., thus reducing the average
annual alpha from 1.0% to 0.8%.9

MVE/GDP in historical context
Although the MVE/GDP ratio tends to exhibit a considerable degree of mean-reversion, its
tendency to mean-revert has arguably declined in the recent past. In this context, it is worth
noting that a regime shift appears to have manifested around the turn of the millennium, as
virtually all MVE/GDP readings in the upper quintile for the country-average occurred in
the period since around 2000, coinciding with the internet bubble of the late-1990s and the
subprime bubble in the early-2000s as well as the subsequent recovery.
The possibility of a higher equilibrium MVE/GDP ratio is in line with Carlson, Pelz and
Wohar (2002) who employ breakpoint tests on the means of valuation ratios, providing statis-
tical evidence for an upward shift of average price-earnings ratios. Similarly, Lansing (2017)
shows that lower risk-free rates, as measured by r-star, along with the growth rate of poten-
tial GDP and the core PCE inflation rate explains a significant share of higher CAPE ratios
since the 1980s. Changes in equilibrium valuation levels are not confined to equity markets,
however. Knoll, Schularick and Steger (2017), for instance, document a structural break in
house prices induced by the rising cost of land in the second half of the twentieth century. In
this context it should be noted that the time-series analysis employed in the present study
omits a significant amount of twentieth century financial data for which quarterly data is not
available. As Rajan and Zingales (2003) show, countries have experienced a high time-series

variation in financial development, as proxied by the fraction of stock market capitalisation
to GDP. The main finding of their “great reversals” hypothesis suggests that countries with
highly developed financial sectors were more advanced as measured by market capitalisation
in 1913 than they were in 1980. It was only by the end of the 1990s that the levels reached
in 1913 were exceeded. Before this background we caution that the sample period studied
herein might not be fully representative for the twentieth century, as a dominating period
of the second half of the twentieth century was characterised by what appears to have been
a catch-up to its 1913 levels, for which data is not available. The high MVE/GDP values
since the 1990s might therefore not be as exceptional as they might appear at first sight.
The possibility that a new equilibrium has formed towards the end of the twentieth cen-
tury is substantiated by Orhangazi (2008) and Fasianos, Guevara and Pierros (2016) who
demonstrate that the era of “financialisation” led to an increased importance of the financial
sector and financial markets starting in the 1970s – just as MVE/GDP has begun its long,
and steady march up from approximately 0.1 in the 1970s and early-1980s to almost 0.80 at
the height of the internet bubble. Neither during the subsequent collapse nor at any time
since did the MVE/GDP ratio approach the relatively low readings experienced in the 1970s,
possibly indicating the establishment of a permanently higher equilibrium ratio.


Conclusion
This paper showed that the market value of equities scaled by gross domestic product pos-
sesses statistically significant forecast properties for long-term equity returns for our fourteen-
country dataset, comparable in quality to that of the United States. With R2 values ranging
from 42.0% to 93.7%, the explanatory power of MVE/GDP for forward returns in our dataset
is heterogenous, but with 83.2%, on average, comparable to that for the United States.
The relationship between the market value of equity and economic output is a crude, but
straight-forward way of measuring the degree of resources directed to capital markets vis-à-
vis the ‘real’ economy. MVE/GDP can thus be viewed as a yardstick to investor sentiment
towards stock markets and, by logical extension, towards financial assets in general. As
a main advantage over purely price-based valuation measures, MVE/GDP incorporates a
qualitative (i.e. price) as well as a quantitative (i.e. number of shares) dimension. As an
increasing amount of economic resources is being deployed on capital markets, equity prices
are being driven up without a commensurate increase in “real” economic activity and ex-
pected returns fall. If share prices scaled by GDP fall, on the other hand, expected returns
increase.
The analysis conducted in this paper demonstrated that the market value of equity relative
to gross domestic product provides a useful tool for investors. The metric possesses strong
and robust predictive properties over longer horizons (c. ten years), as market valuations
relative to economic output have tended to mean-revert to a long-term equilibrium since
1973, although the latter half of the observation period has witnessed higher MVE/GDP
ratios than the period until the turn of the millennium. Further research is required to
determine whether this represents a temporary deviation from a long-term equilibrium, or,
in fact, may mark a paradigm shift.

Given the forecast quality of the market value of equity-to-gross domestic product ratio
as demonstrated in this article, MVE/GDP constitutes an additional input factor for the
purpose of equity market valuation and the formation of long-term return expectations.
MVE/GDP should not replace conventional valuation measures, such as the cyclically-
adjusted price-to-earnings ratio, however. As Campbell and Shiller (1998) point out, con-
ventional valuation measures (i.e. price-to dividend and cyclically-adjusted price-to-earnings
ratio) deserve a special place among forecasting variables due to the high-quality and long
availability of data, going back to 1871 for the United States. Rather, MVE/GDP should
be seen as a complementary valuation tool by providing incremental information on a non-
earnings-based basis. The demonstrably higher explanatory power of P/E10 for the purpose
of forecasting excess returns makes a convincing case for using MVE/GDP and P/E10 con-
comitantly.
Given current valuation levels, the likelihood of subdued returns or pronounced market
losses over the next ten years is substantial. MVE/GDP thus corroborates the somewhat
sobering return expectations derived from the (raw) Shiller P/E, according to which forward
returns will be an order of magnitude smaller than they have been over the past decade.
MVE/GDP lends further support to the depressed longer-term return forecasts derived from
elevated Shiller P/E ratios. Valuation levels comparable to those today have led to annualised
forward returns over the subsequent ten years in the lower single-digit range, and occasionally
even in negative territory. Current valuations certainly warrant caution, although depressed
levels of interest rates globally might prove continuously supportive of equity prices in spite
of valuation levels in the historical upper extreme.
