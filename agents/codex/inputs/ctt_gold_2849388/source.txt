                 CTT infuses inefficiency into Gold futures trading: An empirical Analysis
                                                          By
           Dr.Velmurugan.PS, Fulbright Fellow, Asst.Professor, Dept.of Commerce, Pondicherry University
                                                          &
             Rinku Champramary, Doctoral Research Scholar, Dept.of Commerce, Pondicherry University



The rationale behind introduction of CTT by government of India is still unclear. But as per few experts, revenue
generation was the topmost priority behind the imposition of CTT, besides checking price volatility. In this study
by adopting the bootstrap, and modified GARCH methodology, we assessed the impact of CTT in non-agriculture
futures commodities with special reference to gold futures traded in MCX. The study finds that CTT has increased
the price volatility in gold futures and has significantly reduced the turnover in trading volume, weakening the
market to great extent. The imposition of CTT not only affected the gold futures market but also contributed to
the inefficiency of the market as the market has still not yet been able to digest the shock that it had received after
the implementation of CTT. As per the revenue generation is concerned, if sole purpose for introducing CTT in
the commodity futures market was to increase the revenue, than it is not suitable for development of commodity
futures market, because      CTT has already increased the transaction cost, and market players have already
responded to it as volume came down approximately to 43% to 53%. We conclude that CTT has direct impact on
policy aspirations as there is a need to transform Indian markets into ‘price setter’ from being ‘price takers.



Key words: Gold futures, Commodity Transaction Tax, Price Taker, Fiscal Deficit, Bootstrap, MGARCH

1. Introduction:
   The levying of Commodity Transaction Tax (CTT) for non-agriculture commodities in India has been under
   debate since its inception. It is a derived version of securities transaction tax, which dates way back to Keynes
   19361 and Tobin 19722. Since then its importance has been growing and spreading gradually under various forms
   to different continents across the globe3. As transaction tax is of great importance to various stake holders, be it
   policy makers or the investors, the administration may like to raise more revenue for the government or to reduce
   the speculation, by imposing it. On the other hand such transaction tax may go against the investors and other
   market participants. Due to such conflicting interests among the stake holders, experts debate on its probable
   implications with supportive theories and empirical evidence to show the pros and cons of levying such financial
   transaction tax. Several of such proponents of financial transaction tax argue that transaction taxes will reduce the
   excess volatility through minimizing speculations and encouraging long term investment4. But, as well said, every
   merit has its own demerit. Many opponents also argue that such tax on financial transaction will bring down the
   securities values, liquidity etc. thus reducing market efficiency and driving the investors away to other offshore
   markets5.
   In India CTT was first proposed in the union budget 2008-09 @ of 0.017% but failed to succeed due to strong
   opposition from different point of view expressed by various stake holder. But such apprehension did not last
   long enough. Again in 28th February 2013, the then finance minister P Chidambaram came with fresh proposal in
   the union budget 2013-14, to levy @0.01 percent CTT for futures contract on non-agriculture commodities traded
   in Indian markets, applicable only to the sellers in the future contracts. Thus India finally succeeded in attaining
   a second place in the world next to Taiwan to impose commodity transaction tax without further validating the
   causes and consequences of commodity futures trading.

  The main proponents of commodity transaction tax in India Shri. P. Chidambaram’s rationale behind introducing
  CTT was to bring commodity market on par with the securities market where a securities transaction tax is levied6.
  The rationale behind saying that there is “no distinction between securities and commodity derivatives trading’
  has already sideline the economic functions of commodity derivatives Viz. Price discovery and risk management
  and in doing so, is likely to deter commodity trading on Indian exchanges and shift transactions to lower cost
  exchanges overseas, or to illegal markets. As trade volume decreases, so too will any revenues generated by the
  CTT” (Charles M. Seeger, J.D, (2013).

  Commodities further differ from securities because a “commodity exchange is clearly not a stock exchange”
  (Pavaskar & Ghosh, 2008) because of their difference in objectives. The main motive of the stock market players
  is to make profits from the rising stock values and investment in it. But this is not so in the case of commodity
  futures market because the main purpose in this market is to hedge the price risks in both the falling and the rising
  markets, depending on their commitments in the physical markets7. Thus opponents are of the view that the
  imposition of commodity transaction tax in futures markets in India will hamper the price discovery and hedging
  mechanism that are inherent characteristics of commodity futures market.

  In this background this study tries to find out the effect of CTT on Indian non-agriculture commodity futures
  market, after the announcement of imposing (CTT) from 1st July 2013 onwards. The papers attempts to put
  forward the market behavior before and after the imposition of such (CTT), analyzing the changes in Trading
  Volume, Trading Lots, Market Return and market efficiency after the introduction of CTT in India.

  The study is dealt in following way: Section 2 gives the overview of commodity futures market in India. Section
  3 gives the review, Section 4 the data and methodology adopted, Section 5: explains the empirical findings. And
  Section 6 outline the conclusions and discussions.

be negative. Similarly, (Umlauf, 1993) finds that the introduction of, or increase in, the Swedish transaction tax
would lead to an increase in stock market price volatility. Examining the effect of changes in transaction taxes in
four Asian countries, (Hu, 1998)Hu finds insignificant impact of transaction taxes on price volatility, but
significant negative impact on stock returns in some countries. (Saporta & Kan, 1997) investigate the effect of
the UK stamp duty on the volatility of continuously compounded 15-minute returns over daily, weekly and
monthly time periods. The results reveal that there is no evidence of any kind of effect, positive or negative, on
the volatility.
There is also empirical evidence that imposition of /increase in STT would decrease return. Hu (1998) finds that
upon the announcement of the tax, the average return fell 1.57 per cent in Taiwan and by 0.55 per cent in Korea.
(Umlauf, 1993) estimates that the Swedish All-Share equity index fell 2.2 per cent when the 1 per cent tax was
announced in 1983 and by 0.8 per cent when the tax rate was increased in 1986. In the UK, (Saporta & Kan, 1997)
report that the equity index declined by 3.33 per cent on the announcement of an increase in stamp duty rate from
1 per cent to 2 per cent. When the stamp duty was decreased from 2 per cent to 1 per cent and then from 1 per
cent to 0.5 per cent, the index increased by 0.558 per cent and 1.054 per cent respectively.

Conclusions:

Several countries which adopted financial transaction tax such as STT have witnessed its negative effects on the
growth and development of the financial markets. Results of this paper shows that India too had been experiencing
such effects, emphasizing the need to act accordingly to ensure that the CTT is repealed and the commodity
futures market is allowed to function in a fair and orderly manner. The results of the study reveals that, contrary
to what the common proponents of transaction taxes claim such as (Summers & Summers, 1989), the market
volatility witnesses an increased after CTT has been introduced in India’s gold futures market. Imposition of CTT
has affected all the stakeholders in non-agriculture commodity futures market, particularly in gold futures evident
from the declining trend in open interest, trading value, turnover ratios etc. The comparative chart of Bullion and
agro- commodity trading value depicts the heat the bullion commodity is facing because of CTT. The Gold
Futures market had witness decrease in turnover in trading value indirectly affecting the liquidity, also increasing
volatility in gold futures return which is consistent with findings in (Umlauf, 1993) and (Jones & Seguin, 1997)
etc. This shows that CTT is not desirable or is contrary to the policy objectives of existence of healthy futures
trading in commodities and its potential impact on policy aspirations in transforming Indian markets into ‘price
setter’ from being ‘price taker. If the government sole motive of introducing commodities transaction tax was to
generate more revenue to its exchequer than we think that it is a big blunder. If we look at the history of financial
transaction tax right from the congressional budget estimates in U.S in 1990s and estimates in japan we can see
