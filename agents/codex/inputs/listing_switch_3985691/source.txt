   Trade-off Between Trade Efficiency and Immediacy:
         Evidence from Switching to NASDAQ


                                            Abstract
     The trade-off between execution immediacy and price improvement has explained the
     choice of traders between dark or lit markets. We show that this choice is altered
     by the same trade-off resulted from trading in dealer versus auction markets. We
     find that the switch to NASDAQ increases execution cost, partly due to the higher
     preferencing agreement in NASDAQ, and in response, uninformed trades, which are
     more sensitive to cost changes, move to dark markets. Our results show that the
     informational efficiency improves in the lit market at the expense of lower liquidity,
     when a firm moves from NYSE to NASDAQ.

1.      Introduction

     Tse and Devos (2004) show that moving from NYSE to NASDAQ increases the trading
cost. In this paper, we find the same negative effect on quoted bid-ask spreads when a firm
moves to NASDAQ, and show that the increase in the execution cost alters the price-time
equilibrium between dark and lit markets. Following the move to NASDAQ, uninformed
trades, who are more sensitive to trade cost, are siphoned from lit market to dark pools
to alleviate the rise in the execution cost at the expense of lower trade immediacy. We
find that the shift of trades to dark markets increases dark liquidity, measured by relative
effective spread. Further, we show that the relative realized spread decreases in the lit
market, indicating an improve in price discovery.
     Cohen, Maier, Schwartz, and Whitcomb (1981) explain one of the several price-time
trade-offs before the traders when a trader decides to use a market order versus a limit
order. If traders have to trade fast on a private information, they are expected to use market
orders, while if they are patient and care more about price improvement and low cost trade,
they will choose limit orders. The trade-off between price and time also occurs in the choice
of a trading venue between auction and dealer markets. Aggarwal and Angel (1997) argue
that firms face a trade-off between the lower cost of an auction market and the marketing
advantage of a dealer market. Auction markets, such as NYSE, have a lower execution
cost and higher price improvement as compared to dealer markets, such as NASDAQ. In
dealer markets, though, the presence of dealers improves immediacy of order execution, as
dealers are almost always there to take a position. One of the major reasons for the high
trading cost in dealer markets is the prevalence of preferencing. Preferencing agreements
between market centers and brokers who route customers’ orders allow market centers to
obtain order flow regardless of their quotes and this may result in a lack of competition in
the market, and even though alternative market centers, whether NYSE or NASDAQ, can
compete for order flow by posting aggressive quotes, they obtain significant portion of their
orders through preferencing. These agreements are much more prevalent in NASDAQ due

                                                1

                  Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=3985691
to its dealer market nature. Weston (2000) argues that the 1997 NASDAQ reforms have
reduced NASDAQ dealers’ rents, however, He, Odders-White, and Ready (2006) document
a persisting high preferencing for NASDAQ stocks and find that dealers in NASDAQ capture
additional profits of up to 55% of the effective spread.
   In addition to the aforementioned trade-off mechanisms, Menkveld, Yueshen, and Zhu
(2017) introduce another price-time trade-off by arguing that after investors receive ur-
gency or cost shocks in real-time, they move between low-cost, low-immediacy venues (dark
venues) and high-cost, high-immediacy venues (exchanges). This pecking order theory and
its predecessors, such as Zhu (2014), explain how the choice between price improvement and
informational advantage segments the market between dark and lit. Zhu (2014) argues that
exchanges are more attractive to informed traders, and dark pools are more attractive to
uninformed traders, referring in part to the higher sensitivity of uninformed traders to trade
cost. In the arrival of a cost shock, uninformed traders are more motivated to move from
lit to dark, resulting in price discovery improvement as well as liquidity deterioration in
exchange (lit) markets. In this paper, we show how the change in the dealer-auction market
equilibrium can affect the dark-lit market equilibrium, and how liquidity and price discovery
are affected in the new equilibrium. When a firm switches from NYSE (an auction market)
to NASDAQ (a dealer market), it creates a cost shock to the traders, both informed and
uninformed. More sensitive to the cost shock, the uninformed traders decide to leave the
exchange and move to dark venues to relieve the cost shock. Thus, the liquidity in the lit
market decreases while informational efficiency and price discovery improve.
   Establishing the mechanism, we take a step back and discuss whether this mechanism
can support the decision of the managers to switch from NYSE to NASDAQ, along with
other reasons expressed in the previous papers, such as Dang, Michayluk, and Pham (2018)
and Tse and Devos (2004). We test the classical explanations stated by the firms’ managers
in their switch announcements. Our tests show that while the market reaction to switch an-
nouncement is insignificant, liquidity and trade volume are negatively affected by the switch,


                                                2

                  Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=3985691
with relatively large magnitude. We find insignificant increase in coverage of analysts and
insignificant decrease in institutional investors’ concentration and capital raise. Additionally,
we find no systematic industry effect that could motivate a decision to move on the basis
of joining a ”club” of, say, tech firms on NASDAQ. We also examine CEO restlessness as
a behavioral reason for the switch; however, we find no significant results. Therefore, we
conjecture that managers choose to switch to NASDAQ to increase price discovery at the
expense of execution cost, following our proposed price-time trade-offs mechanism.
     Our paper makes several important contributions to the optimal trading choice and dark
pools trading literature. It is in line with Jiang, McInish, and Upson (2012), who find that
the price discovery on exchanges improves in fragmented markets because uninformed traders
are able to route their order flow to off-exchange trading venues, leaving a larger proportion
of informed traders at the exchange.
     The rest of the paper follows this structure: In Section 2, we review the literature on
dark pools and on comparison between NYSE and NASDAQ, and propose our hypotheses.
Section 3 explains the data and variable. Section 4 presents our analyses and results. In
section 5, we examine several rival explanations for switching to NASDAQ. Finally, Section
6 concludes.



2.      Dark Trades, Listing Switch, and Hypotheses De-
        velopment

2.1.    Dark vs. Lit Trades

     Dark pools are one of the forms of ATS (Alternative Trading Systems) markets, in which
trade transparency is less than in exchange markets. Dark and lit (exchange) trades differ
in their levels of pre-trade transparency, but not post-trade transparency (Comerton-Forde
and Putniņš (2015)). Pre-trade transparency is the degree to which information is available
to market participants about buying or selling interest, including quotes to buy or sell and

                                                 3

                   Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=3985691
the volumes available at the quotes. Post-trade transparency is the information about trades
after they execute. A considerable fraction of trade volume belongs to dark pools. 14% of the
consolidated volume trades in 19 dark pools in the US. In Europe, 16 dark markets account
for approximately 4.5% of the volume, and in Canada, dark pools represent 2% of the volume
(Buti, Rindi, and Werner (2017)). The big development in dark pools came in 2007 when the
SEC passed Regulation NMS (National Market System), which allowed investors to bypass
public exchanges to gain price improvements. The goal was to attract a number of new
players to the market, and, as a result, a large number of dark pools were created over the
next ten years. It is fair to say that there are three main features differentiate lit markets
from dark venues, which are partially exempt from the NMS requirements. First, compared
with lit markets, dark venues currently are not subject to NMS fair-access requirements and
thus can prohibit or limit clients from accessing their services. Second, dark venues provide
limited or no pre-trade transparency in that their best-priced bids and offers are not required
for inclusion in the publicly distributed consolidated quotation data. Finally, executions on
dark venues occur at finer price increments than on lit markets.
   There is a considerable body of theoretical and empirical research on the choice of traders
to trade in dark markets or exchanges. Dark pools are required to match orders at prices
within the National Best Bid and Offer (NBBO), while exchange orders can be submitted
to execute immediately even if the resulting execution prices fall outside of the prevailing
NBBO. While this approach in dark pools can lead to lower bid-ask spread costs, if no
match within the NBBO exists, then no trade will occur. Menkveld et al. (2017) proposes the
pecking order hypothesis which predicts that as investors’ trading needs become more urgent,
they move from low-cost, low-immediacy venues to high-cost, high-immediacy venues. More
concretely, recent theories of dark pools mentioned above predict that dark venues (low-cost,
low-immediacy) are at the top of the pecking order, whereas lit venues are at the bottom
(high-cost, high-immediacy), because dark venues provide potential price improvement but
lack execution certainty. In another seminal theory papers, Zhu (2014) proposes a model


                                                 4

                   Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=3985691
of venue choice that allows both informed and uninformed traders to trade on exchanges
and dark pools. Based on the model, since informed traders trade in the same direction
and crowd one side of the market, they face a higher execution risk (larger implied bid-ask
spread, indicating a higher execution cost) in a dark pool than uninformed traders face.
Therefore, exchanges will be relatively attractive to informed traders, and dark pools will be
relatively attractive to uninformed traders. Consistently, Jiang et al. (2012) and Foley and
Putniņš (2016) also show that the traders in dark pools are mainly uninformed. Jiang et al.
(2012) find that the price discovery on exchanges improves in fragmented markets because
uninformed traders are able to route their order flow to off-exchange trading venues, leaving
a larger proportion of informed traders at the exchange.


2.2.   Switching to NASDAQ and Its Effect on Dark Trades

   The choice of a stock listing market is a relatively permanent one. Once firms choose to
IPO on one market, they generally remain listed on that market indefinitely. All the same,
if a switch occurred, it was exclusively from NASDAQ to NYSE. However, Aeroflex Co. was
the first company to change its listing to NASDAQ in March 2000. The economic effects of
this change have been explored with mixed results. Kalay and Portniaguina (2001) found a
significant positive abnormal return, and a reduce in the daily bid-ask spread and the trading
volume; On the contrary, Pruitt, Van Ness, and Van Ness (2002) found no improvement in
trading or quoting behavior, but a deterioration in key metrics. These conflicting results
might explain the dearth of follow-on switches in the following years. Nevertheless, in 2008,
the market recorded a jump from essentially none to almost 50 firms opting out of NYSE and
switching to NASDAQ. In 2009, despite the market-wide drought in listings in the aftermath
of the crisis, firms kept on switching at an unprecedented number, from NYSE to NASDAQ.
Switches continued in the following years and, since 2010, the numbers of switches between
the two markets in the two directions have been essentially the same (excluding ETFs and
ETNs). Figure (1) illustrates the distribution of switches over the years for both directions.

   Although the weight of literature is larger on the switches from NASDAQ to NYSE,
there are very few papers that find benefits in switching to NASDAQ, and try to explain
the rationale. Among them, Cheon, Christensen, and Smith Bamber (2001) show that
abnormal returns around the earning announcements are larger for firms listed on NASDAQ
compared to those on NYSE, and Masulis and Shivakumar (2002) compare the speed of price
adjustments to seasoned equity offering announcements by NYSE/AMEX and NASDAQ,
and show that NASDAQ is faster. Execution times for orders in NASDAQ stocks are lower
than for orders in NYSE-listed stocks, also consistent with Boehmer (2005)’s results. Speed
is presumably an important aspect of execution quality to traders with short investment
horizons and to investors who base trading decisions on time-sensitive information (He et al.
(2006)). The most recent papers that study the aspects of switch to NASDAQ find an
increase in execution cost upon the switch. Tse and Devos (2004) show that firms that
move to NASDAQ increase investor recognition and volume at the expense of an increase
in trading costs. Dang et al. (2018) find the move to the NASDAQ increases trading costs,
while improves visibility and attracts more liquidity providers in the long term.
   Few papers have investigated the level of dark trades in the two groups of NYSE-listed
stocks and the NASDAQ-listed ones. Buti, Rindi, and Werner (2011) show that dark pool
activity is generally higher for NASDAQ-listed stocks than NYSE-listed ones. They attribute
this difference in dark pool activity to the type of stocks listed and mainly traded in each
of these two exchanges. They show that there is an association between dark pool activity
and firm size (or stock capitalization).
   The above arguments lead us to propose the following hypotheses: When a firm moves


                                                 6

                   Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=3985691
to NASDAQ, the preferencing friction increases and therefore the cost of trade (in the entire
market, including both lit and dark) increases. Accordingly, we expect that upon the switch
the quoted bid-ask spread widens:
     Hypothesis 1) Following the switch to NASDAQ, execution cost (quoted bid-ask spread)
increases.
     Since the cost of trade is increased, uninformed traders, who are more sensitive to cost
changes, decide to move to a less expensive markets, namely dark pools:
     Hypothesis 2) Following the switch to NASDAQ, a significant volume moves from lit
markets to dark pools.
     The asymmetry of sensitivity with respect to the execution cost between informed and un-
informed traders segments the market and increases the relative number of informed traders
in the lit market. Since more informed traders have moved to the dark markets, its liquidity
improves. We decompose the quoted bud-ask spread to effective and realized spreads to have
a more precise measure for the hidden liquidity (effective spread) in the dark pools and price
efficiency (realized spread) in the lit markets:
     Hypothesis 3) After switching to NASDAQ, the liquidity (effective spread) in the dark
market improves, the liquidity in the lit market deteriorates, and the informational efficiency
(realized spread) in the lit market improves.
     In the rest of the paper, we test these hypotheses.

primarily on the basis of their quoted bid-ask spreads. Along with this measure, we also use
two other measures for execution quality: effective and realized spreads. If trades can occur

trade price. Effective spread is given by the signed difference of the trade price minus the
midpoint of the consolidated best bid or offer at the time of order receipt (Equation (1)).

quoted spread reflects disseminated quotes, the effective spread reflects trades at implicit
quotes not publicly disseminated. Therefore, it is an appropriate proxy for the liquidity
component of the execution cost. To control for the magnitude of the price, relative effective

   According to Zhu (2014), uninformed traders are more sensitive to trade cost and prefer
to trade in dark markets when the trade cost in exchanges is high. Therefore, we conjecture

4.4.    Switch to NASDAQ and Market Reaction

     The transfer of uninformed traders to dark would have an implication: lower adverse
selection cost in the lit market, although the aggregate quoted bid-ask would increase, due
to lower dealer/broker competition in NASDAQ. To see how market reacts to this trade-off
between cost and market efficiency, we run an event study around the switch announcement
date. We estimate a standard market-adjusted model with a 180 days estimation window
of (-210;-30). Abnormal returns and Cumulative Abnormal Returns (CAR) are computed
from 5 days before the announcement day up to 10 days after the announcement. Figure
(2) presents cross-sectional averages of the cumulative abnormal market returns around the
switch announcement date at 95% confidence interval. CARs are insignificantly different
from zero in either direction, confirming that investors see the switch as a trade-off with no
significant value creation.

5.      Why Do Firms Switch to NASDAQ?

     Finding the reasons that motivate the switch in either direction has become even more
difficult. In recent years, NYSE and NASDAQ have converged in their type of microstruc-
ture and quality, as well as requirements and fees for listing. Weston (2000) shows that
market reforms on NASDAQ mitigated the differences in spreads between the NASDAQ and
NYSE. Although NYSE used to be, by a considerable margin, a more prestigious and more
disciplined market, which was only open to ”mature” companies, than NASDAQ, nowadays
the services and qualities, in terms of price discovery and liquidity, are almost the same for
both. Among many reforms on the two exchanges, one can refer to the two that paved the
way for the listed firms to switch from one to the other: first, NYSE became a hybrid market
in early 2007 due to the increasing popularity of electronic trading. Second, in July 2007, the


                                                 15

                   Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=3985691
SEC approved a measure under which companies with a three-letter symbol on one exchange
could migrate to a different exchange and maintain that symbol. This regulatory change is
important because, from the investor’s point of view, changing ticker involves changing all
the databases and making the new data compatible with the previous one. Also, it would re-
quire an extra effort for marketing and informing (mostly non-institutional) investors about
the new ticker. Among the similarities that the two markets have converged to, fees and
direct costs are of importance to study. Corwin and Harris (2001) show that although the
direct issue costs of listing in NYSE is higher, total issue costs (including underpricing) do
not differ across NYSE and NASDAQ and are unlikely to affect the listing decision. Macey
and O’Hara (2002) study the economics of listing fees on NYSE. The immediate listing cost,
which consists of a fixed (entry or listing fee) and a variable (annual fee linked to the number
of shares outstanding) portion, is not significantly different between the two exchanges. In
more detail, NASDAQ has three different tiers (NASDAQ (2017a)): The NASDAQ Global
Select Market, The NASDAQ Global Market, and The NASDAQ Capital Market. Gover-
nance requirements are the same across all three tiers, while financial requirements are most
stringent in The NASDAQ Global Select Market. The entry fee ranges from 75,000$ to
250,000$ depending on the number of total shares outstanding. Also, the annual fee has a
range from 42,000$ to 155,000$. In NYSE, the entry (listing) fee is in the range of 100,000$
to 150,000$, and the annual fee ranges from 30,000$ to 85,000$ (NYSE (2017)). While these
numbers apply to common shares, the rates change for other securities, such as preferred
shares and structured products. Overall, the difference in fees between the two exchanges
is relatively small and not large enough to compensate for the direct and indirect switching
costs.
   Despite the similarities and convergence, we see migrations of firms from one venue to
the other, in almost the same number. There have been a number of papers and research
to study the benefits of switch in each direction and try to explain the managers’ decision.
Some papers analyzed the switches regardless of their directions. Webb (1999) analyzes the


                                                 16

                   Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=3985691
timing for switch, and shows that managers switch immediately after a good performance,
and that performance tends to become poorer after the switch. Some papers propose differ-
ences between asset pricing models in the two exchanges. Goyal, Pérignon, and Villa (2008)
demonstrate the absence of complete similarity in return factors between NYSE and NAS-
DAQ. They show that, although common factors exist, there are exchange-specific factors
that systematically affect performance. Kaul, Mehrotra, and Stefanescu (2006) show that
when firms move from NASDAQ to NYSE, the stock price process begins to co-move with the
NYSE index, and its co-movement with the NASDAQ index declines. This result is at odds
with fundamentals-based co-movement and is in line with the habitat-based co-movement
of Barberis, Shleifer, and Wurgler (2005). It supports that moving to NYSE generally has
positive effects on stock pricing and efficiency. One important implication is that moving
to NASDAQ should conversely generate adverse effects. This is, however, at odds with the
recent evidence. Cheng and Stunda (2015) propose that NASDAQ listing has a positive
effect on short term returns, while NYSE listed firms have a better long term investment
payoff.
   The extant literature on the listing choice focuses primarily on companies switching
to NYSE, and covers the period prior to NYSE’s transition to a hybrid market. Most of
the papers provide evidence of a positive market reaction and a liquidity and volatility
improvement favoring the switch from NASDAQ to NYSE. Jain and Kim (2006) show that
firms that switch to NYSE experience positive cumulative abnormal returns due to the
increase in their visibility. Atkins and Dyl (1997) show that trading volume decreases for
those companies switching their listing from NASDAQ to NYSE, but this can be attributed
to the absence of dealers in NYSE at the time of the study. In an effort to explain the positive
effects of switching to NYSE, Rao, Davis, and Ward (2000) identified embeddedness, social
identity, and ties to in-group and out-group affiliation as common factors that positively
affected switching firms. In particular, they show that firms with growth potential are usually
listed on NASDAQ. Frieder and Suk (2006) show that firms listed on NYSE are more likely


                                                 17

                   Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=3985691
to disclose earning forecasts. Lin and Liao (2015) study the manager’s financial reporting
behavior in relation to exchange switching and show that moving to a more disciplined
exchange mitigates the manager’s incentive to inflate the financial reporting. Kedia and
Panchapagesan (2011) show that cost of capital decreases by moving to NYSE due to better
visibility and liquidity, and firms take this action before raising debt or equity or before an
acquisition. Baker, Powell, and Weaver (1998) show that, despite the belief that switching
from NASDAQ to NYSE increases media visibility, in the short term, the change in the
visibility is not significant. However, in the long term, after switching, small- and medium-
size firms gain more visibility. They show that the increase in media coverage is more related
to rapid earnings growth before the switch than it is to the change in listing.
   In this final section, we complete the studies by Tse and Devos (2004) and Dang et al.
(2018) and examine other rival hypotheses to further explain the switch to NASDAQ. Similar
to Dang et al. (2018) and in order to gain insight about the drivers behind the decision to
switch, we looked at the reasons stated by the firms at the time of the announcement of
the switch. We manually downloaded all top managers’ interviews, firms’ press releases, and
statements on the company websites published at the time of the announcement or in the
immediate vicinity (+10 days) from Factiva. Given the heterogeneity of the narratives, we
have asked two research assistants to classify the motivations into thirteen categories and
then compared the classification results. The two classifications were extremely coherent,
and the few discrepancies have been resolved by the authors. Figure (3) reports the results
of this exercise. The most commonly reported reasons for a listing switch include visibility,
peer affiliation, appeal to institutional investors, cost reduction, improvement in liquidity
and volatility, and, finally, the use of technological services.

     CEO Restlessness. Behavioral Finance proposes the possibility of irrational decisions by

     To conclude, we do not find any strong rival explanation against our trade-off hypothesis.
Therefore, we propose price-time trade-off as another significant reason, along with other
proposed explanations in the literature, for the rationale of the switch to NASDAQ.



6.      Conclusion

     In this paper, we showed a substitute for the trade-off between trading in auction and
dealer markets. We showed that once the firms change their listing from an auction market
(NYSE) to a dealer market (NASDAQ), the traders adjust their trade venue in other seg-
ments of the market, namely dark and lit venues. We showed that upon the switch from
NYSE to NASDAQ, trades shift from lit (exchange) markets to dark (off-exchange) ones.

                                                  20

                    Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=3985691
As a consequence, the liquidity in the dark market increases and in the lit market decreases.
However, the price discovery in the lit market improves at the expense of lower liquidity.
   We propose that managers choose to switch to NASDAQ to increase price discovery at
the expense of execution cost. While our other alternative hypotheses do not show sound
empirical supports, the trade-off hypothesis can partially explain the rationale of the switch,
along with other previous explanations in the literature.
