                                               Abstract
        Trend-following strategies, rooted in time-series momentum, have demonstrated enduring
   efficacy across diverse asset classes and market conditions. This paper provides a comprehen-
   sive exploration of the theoretical foundations, risk-return dynamics, and practical implemen-
   tation of such strategies. Key findings reveal that trend following strategies exhibit convex
   return profiles with “crisis alpha” properties, thriving during market dislocations while of-
   fering diversification benefits. The choice of time scale for trend calculation is paramount, as
   strategies must align with an asset’s return characteristics. Empirical simulations and com-
   parative analyses of trend measures underscore that methodology matters less than selecting
   an appropriate time horizon.
        Empirical evidence from China’s futures markets (1999–2019) confirms the global appli-
   cability of trend following, with a combined multi-scale strategy achieving a 16.24% annual-
   ized return and a Sharpe ratio of 0.88, significantly outperforming buy-and-hold approaches.
   However, challenges persist, including periods of underperformance linked to heightened as-
   set correlations, reduced market trends, and leverage sensitivity. Critically, the strategy’s
   success hinges on disciplined risk management and acceptance of short-term drawdowns. By
   synthesizing academic insights and practical considerations, this study reaffirms trend follow-
   ing as a resilient, albeit imperfect, tool for portfolio diversification and crisis hedging, urging
   practitioners to prioritize time-scale alignment and robust risk controls.

1         Introduction
        In quantitative investment, it is often said that the trend is your friend. Trend following is
perhaps the most easily understood quantitative investment strategy. When the price of an asset
rises, it is in an upward trend, and one might consider buying long; when the price of an asset
falls, it is in a downward trend, and one might consider selling short. Therefore, trend following is
essentially a time-series momentum strategy, focusing solely on the historical performance of the
asset itself, without considering the performance of other assets at the current time. In contrast,
another concept is cross-sectional momentum, with classic research from Jegadeesh and Titman
(1993). At a given time t, one computes the performance of all assets over a past period, and
goes long on a basket of relatively strong performing assets (winners) and goes short a basket of
relatively weak performing assets (losers). Time-series momentum is an absolute concept, while
cross-sectional momentum is a relative concept.
        When people talk about trend following, they often refer to time-series momentum. This
article will mainly focus on time-series momentum. In the following discussion, we will use the
terms trend following and time-series momentum interchangeably1 . For those interested in cross-
sectional momentum, please refer to the related literature regarding the momentum factor in
empirical asset pricing.


1.1        Trend Following is Everywhere
        As a quantitative strategy, trend following is ubiquitous. Moskowitz et al. (2012) construct
a time-series momentum portfolio using 58 assets including commodity futures, bond futures,
currency futures, and stock index futures. They found that within a range of 1 to 12 months,
returns are persistent, meaning assets that have risen in the past are more likely to rise in the
future, and assets that have fallen in the past are more likely to fall in the future. From 1985 to
2009, the time-series momentum portfolio performed excellently, with significant excess returns
greater than 0.
        Hurst et al. (2017) expand the research scope and time period to include 29 commodities, 11
equity indices, 15 bond indices, and 12 currency pairs, with empirical data starting from 18802 .
When constructing the trend following portfolio, they considered three different frequencies: 1
month, 3 months, and 12 months. From 1880 to 2013, the trend following portfolio achieved an
annualized return of 14.9%, and even after considering a 2% management fee and 20% performance
fee, it still achieved an annualized return of 11.2%, with an annualized volatility of only 9.7%.

1.2       Underlying Logic
      The long-term vitality of trend following must be supported by solid logical foundations. As
all history is in-sample, therefore when evaluating whether an investment strategy holds water, if
one focuses too much on risk-return metrics like returns or Sharpe ratios without paying attention
to why it works, even the most impressive data can be fragile.
      Academically, when explaining the source of factor returns, two perspectives are often consid-
ered: risk compensation and mispricing. The former is from the perspective of classical finance,
suggesting that an anomaly can achieve excess returns because it bears some systematic risk, and
the excess returns are compensation for that risk; the latter is from the perspective of behav-
ioral finance, suggesting that either some constraint prevents the anomaly from disappearing, or
investor cognitive biases lead to irrational behavior.
      Moskowitz et al. (2012) study the source of trend following returns from a risk perspective.
Time-series regression results showed that trend following cannot be explained by existing risk
factors (such as HML, SMB, etc.); not only is it difficult to identify the source of risk, but
the authors also find that trend following performs well when the market performs poorly, such
as during the 2008 financial crisis when (Commodity Trading Advisor) CTA funds stood out.
Therefore, it is difficult to understand trend following from a risk compensation perspective. It is
much easier to discuss from a behavioral finance perspective, as Clare et al. (2016) and Hurst et al.
(2017) have done in detail. The efficient market hypothesis suggests that when important new
information appears, prices will immediately react to match the latest intrinsic value. However,
due to investor cognitive biases, the market is not completely efficient but slowly absorbs the
impact of new information.
      Generally, the formation of a trend can be divided into three stages, as shown in Figure 13 . In
the initial stage, due to anchoring effects and disposition effects, prices underreact to information.

        to quickly change their minds, leading to slow reactions to new information and thus slow
        price reactions.

       Disposition Effect: Investors tend to sell profitable stocks too early to realize gains; con-
        versely, they are reluctant to cut losses on losing stocks. The consequence is that prices do

   Once a trend starts, it enters the second stage: trend continuation or even overreaction, often

    Herding Effect: Also known as the bandwagon effect, when investors’ views differ from the

     their views, allowing the price trend to continue.

   Finally, trends do not last forever, and prices do not move in one direction indefinitely. In the
end, prices may have overreacted, deviating significantly from fundamentals, leading to a reversal
and the end of the trend.

2         Risk-Return Characteristics

2.1         Divergent Risk Taking
        Jusselin et al. (2017) point out that the return characteristics of time-series momentum strate-
gies is convex. When the trend is small, the strategy yields negative returns; only when the trend
is significant (either upward or downward) does the strategy generate positive returns.
        According to this analysis, the losses of time-series momentum strategies are limited, while the
potential gains are unbounded. It is worth noting that the limited losses are conditional, requiring
that the strategy does not employ excessive leverage (as high leverage non-linearly amplifies both
gains and losses) and that asset prices are continuous. Examining the PnL curve of trend following
strategies, it is often observed that such a strategy often experiences a series of small drawdowns,
followed by a significant upward movement (a large trend), which offsets the previous drawdowns,
resulting in positive long-term returns. This characteristic of limited losses and unlimited gains
leads to the positive skewness of the strategy’s return distribution. This distribution is also known
as the “Holy Grail” distribution.
        It is precisely this unique characteristic that allows trend following strategies to stand out
when other assets experience tail risks, which is why they are renowned as crisis alpha4 . Why
do different strategies exhibit different distribution characteristics in their returns? This can be

explained by two types of risks. Any investment strategy that aims to generate profits must bear
certain risks. The type of risk a strategy assumes determines the characteristics of its return
distribution. Risks can be broadly categorized into convergent risks and divergent risks, and thus
strategies can also be classified into convergent risk-taking and divergent risk-taking.

    Convergent risks are those that are understood and can be measured through modeling;

    Divergent risks are unknown, unpredictable, and cannot be well-measured risks.

   When constructing a convergent risk-taking strategy, people make investment decisions based
on prior beliefs about the asset’s price movement derived from financial or economic theory. Con-
versely, when constructing a divergent risk-taking strategy, people assume that the future move-
ment of the asset is unknown and make decisions based on the asset’s past price movements
without using any a prior beliefs about whether the asset will rise or fall.
   For example, value investing in the stock market is a representative of convergent risk-taking.
This type of strategy involves in-depth research to uncover the intrinsic value of companies, buying
when the price is below the intrinsic value, and patiently waiting for the price to revert to its
intrinsic value, disregarding short-term fluctuations or even sustained declines. These are the
risks that this type of strategy is willing and actively chooses to bear. On the other hand, trend
following strategies are representatives of divergent risk-taking. These strategies do not make any
judgments about the intrinsic value of the asset but strictly trade based on price movements:
buying when the price rises and selling when the price falls. They gradually increase positions
when profitable and quickly close positions when incurring losses. Because of this, trend following
strategies belong to divergent risk-taking strategies, and their returns exhibit the characteristics
of the holy grail distribution, which is why trend following strategies remain enduringly popular.
   It is necessary to point out that while the return distribution of a trend following strategy
is appealing, achieving such an outcome is extremely challenging. The most difficult aspect of
trend following strategies is holding onto positions during trends to let profits run (as only in this
way can the right-tail of the return distribution be achieved). However, due to cognitive biases,
people tend to have an excessive preference for low returns with high certainty, thus often closing
profitable positions prematurely. Only by overcoming these human errors can one truly enjoy the
benefits of the holy grail distribution provided by trend following strategies.

strategy. Taking the U.S. stock market as an example, as it reflects economic growth and the profit
growth of public companies, the market exhibits a slow bull structure. In such a structure, trend
following strategies that use long-term trend signals perform better than those that use short-term
trend signals.

3.1    Model of Log Return
   The above examples show that any trend following strategy is only applicable to investment
assets whose returns conform to its assumptions. So, what are the general characteristics of
returns for investment assets? How can we use these characteristics to select a suitable trend
following strategy? To explore this, this section decomposes returns into three parts: (long-term)
mean, autocorrelation, and white noise, and studies them through simulations. Reviewing what
we learned in time series analysis lectures, here are the definitions:

    Long-term Mean: It represents the mean of the returns per unit time over the long term.

    Autocorrelation: To simplify the discussion, we only consider the autocorrelation at lag 1
      here; it represents the correlation of returns between two adjacent units of time.

    White Noise: The random fluctuation of returns within each unit of time; the random
      fluctuations at different times are independent of each other.

   Among these three parts, white noise is the random disturbance; for the other two parts,
the higher the long-term mean and the stronger the autocorrelation, the more effective the trend
following strategy will be. So, which of these two factors is more important? How do they
respectively affect the performance of a trend following strategy? To facilitate analysis, we use a
mathematical model to artificially synthesize log returns. These log returns satisfy a predefined
long-term mean µ, autocorrelation ρ, and the volatility of white noise σ. The procedure to generate
the return time series is summarized below:

  1. Initialization: b0 ∼ N (0, σ),

  2. White Noise Sequence: et ∼ N (0, σ) for t = 1, 2, . . .,

  3. Auxiliary Series Generation: bt = ρ · bt−1 + et for t = 1, 2, . . .,

  4. Return Series Construction: rt = bt − µb + µ for t = 0, 1, 2, . . ., where µb is the sample
      mean of bt .

   In the procedure above, et is a white noise series that follows a normal distribution with a
mean of 0 and a standard deviation of σ. rt is the simulated log return series. To ensure that it
satisfies the given parameters µ and ρ, an auxiliary series bt is used in the construction process.
First, the series {bt } is iteratively constructed to satisfy the autocorrelation ρ. Since constructing

To ensure this study is relevant to investment practice, the values of these three parameters need

4     Which Trend Measure?
    On the technical side, there are many ways to measure trends: time series momentum based on
past cumulative returns, moving average crossovers or channel breakouts from technical analysis,
various state space models like Kalman filter, linear regression methods using prices, and even
frequency domain analysis (such as Fourier transform, wavelet analysis, etc.).
    When the backtest results of a particular method are not promising, people’s first reaction is
always to look for another (and usually more complex) method to calculate the trend. However,
at their core, all the above methods are based on the information contained in the time series of
prices or returns. Is there really a method or indicator that significantly outperforms others? This
is the question that concerns this section.

6     The Essence of A Trend Signal
    Trend following is a right-side trading strategy that uses a trend measure to describe the
strength of an upward or downward trend over a past period, assuming that the trend will continue.
Regardless of the trend measure used, we cannot predict when a trend will end in advance, so trend
strategies often incur losses when the trend reverses. This risk-return characteristic is inherent in
trend strategies.
    In this section, we attempt to answer why trend strategies are inherently right-side trading
strategies and why trend signals have a (significant) lag. This explanation begins with the dis-
cussion about the essence of trend signals, using moving averages as an example. Mathematically,
the essence of moving averages is a low-pass filter. Its purpose is to filter out high-frequency dis-
turbances (noise) in a time series while retaining useful low-frequency trends. How can we extract
the true low-frequency trend from a time series? The following mathematical explanation reveals
the cause of the lag in trend signals.

tionally, assume we have a filtering function f () that operates on the time domain t (the specific
form of f is determined by the chosen moving average algorithm).

windows to calculate trends. Additionally, people prefer to use exponential weighting or other
complex weighting methods to capture trend changes more quickly10 , but the lag caused by the
lack of future data makes trend following inherently a right-side trading strategy.

7.1       Low Sharpe Ratio
       Moskowitz et al. (2012) calculate the Sharpe ratios of trend following strategies using a 12-
month trend measure for different asset classes. Although trend following strategies achieve pos-
itive Sharpe ratios across all assets, most of the annualized Sharpe ratios are below 0.4. From
the mainstream (yet irrational) market perspective, such performance is unattractive, but Sharpe
ratios in this range are representative of the performance of trend following strategies on individual
assets.
       While there is limited room for improvement in trend following strategies on individual assets,
the advantage lies in the low correlation among different asset classes. Thus, we can combine
strategies on different assets to achieve diversification and improve risk-return characteristics11 .


7.2       Crisis Alpha
       CTA funds employing trend following strategies gained prominence during the 2008 financial
crisis for their exceptional performance, earning the reputation of a drawdown protector. Corre-
lation analysis reveals that trend following strategies exhibit a low correlation (−0.09) with the
Chinese stock market, providing effective diversification benefits. Beyond diversification, these
strategies excel during market downturns, a feature termed “crisis alpha.” Kaminski (2011) de-
fines crisis alpha as “Profits gained by exploiting persistent trends that occur across markets during
times of crisis.”
       During panic-driven sell-offs, most assets face liquidation, and market sentiment plummets.
Trend-following strategies profit by shorting declining markets. Hamill et al. (2016) analyze trend
following performance during equity and bond market crashes. By sorting quarterly equity and
bond returns into quintiles, they demonstrate that trend following generates significantly higher
returns in the worst-performing quintiles.
       Hurst et al. (2017) evaluate trend following performance during the ten largest U.S. equity
market crises, including the 2008 global financial crisis, the dot-com bubble, and the 1987 crash.
Trend following delivered positive returns in 8 out of 10 crises, with minimal losses in the remaining
two. Allocating 20% of a 60/40 portfolio to trend following enhances Sharpe ratios and reduces
drawdowns.

8.3       Return Persistence
   One of the key points to profitability in trend following is the persistence of returns. Generally,
the better the return persistence, the smoother the trend, and the greater the potential profits for
the strategy. Before constructing the trend following portfolio, we first explore the persistence of
returns across different futures assets.
   The method used is to conduct a panel regression, where the risk-adjusted monthly returns
of all assets are the dependent variable, and the lagged risk-adjusted monthly returns (lagged h
months) are independent variables. The significance of the regression coefficients can be used to
measure return persistence. The panel regression model is:
                                                                                  
                                               rt,s                         rt−h,s
                                                          = α + βh                       + ϵ,                                    (17)
                                               σt,s                         σt−h,s

where rt,s is the return of asset s in month t, σt,s is the realized volatility of asset s at time t, and
h is the number of months lagged.
   The lag period h ranges from 1 to 60 months. For each lag h, a panel regression model is
estimated, and the t-statistic for the regression coefficient is obtained. Intuitively, if the regression
coefficient βh is significantly greater than 0, it suggests good return persistence; if βh is significantly
less than 0, it suggests clear reversal characteristics. The reason for using risk-adjusted returns
is that volatility varies greatly across assets, and using returns without adjustment would lead to
incomparable results. The realized volatility for each asset is calculated using an exponentially
weighted method based on daily returns.
   Figure 23 plots the t-statistics for all lags under consideration. It can be observed that for lags
between 1 and 12 months, the regression coefficients are significantly greater than 0 most of the
time, indicating high return persistence within one year. On the other hand, beyond 12 months,
a reversal pattern becomes more evident, which aligns with the observations of Moskowitz et al.
(2012).

8.4    Portfolio Construction
   Next, following the methods of Moskowitz et al. (2012) and Hurst et al. (2013), we construct
trend following portfolios. At the end of each month t, for each asset s, we calculate its return
over the past h months. The direction of the trade is determined based on the sign of this return:
if positive, the position is long; if negative, the position is short. Specifically, the trend following
return for asset s in month t + 1 is:

                                         T F,s         s       s
                                        rt,t+1 = sign(rt−h,t )rt,t+1 ,                             (18)

       s                                               s
where rt,t+1 is the return of asset s in month t + 1, rt−h,t is the cumulative return of asset s over
the previous h months, and h is the lookback window, with values of 1, 3, and 12 corresponding
to 1 month, 3 months, and 1 year.
   The weight of each asset is determined by the inverse of its volatility. This volatility-inverse
weighting, also known as naive Risk Parity, is a simplified version of risk parity that considers only
the volatility of the individual components, not their correlations. Assets with higher volatility
(such as stock index futures) will have smaller weights, while assets with lower volatility (such
as bond futures) will have larger weights. Inverse volatility weighting helps ensure that the risk
contribution of each asset is balanced, thus providing diversification in the portfolio’s risk sources.
   The leverage for each asset is determined by a fixed target risk, set at an annualized volatility
of 40%. This means the leverage for each asset is adjusted based on its volatility level to achieve
the target risk. The reason for setting the target at 40% is not arbitrary, but to ensure that
the overall trend following portfolio has an annualized volatility of approximately 12%, which
aligns with classical factor models, such as Fama and French (1993)’s three-factor model. After
considering the weights and leverage, the trend following return for asset s in month t + 1 is:

9        Has It Failed?
       In general, combining trend following strategies across multiple assets can (largely) improve
risk-adjusted return, i.e., the Sharpe ratio. However, we must also recognize that multi-asset trend
following strategies can still incur sustained losses in the short to medium term (i.e., 1 to 3 years).
If one looks at the performance of the famous Barclay CTA Index12 , it is clear that its performance
since July 2011 has been rather dismal, with almost continuous drawdowns since 2015.
       Depending on the market conditions, trend following strategies can have periods of good per-
formance and prolonged periods of losses. In the long run, trend following strategies can yield
excellent returns and are worth allocating to. However, those who can truly allocate and manage
it must have a very clear understanding of how poor its performance can be in the short to medium
term. We next discuss this issue from three aspects, i.e., strategy capacity, asset correlation, and
lack of trends.


9.1       Capacity
       The success of trend following in 2008 brought it into the spotlight, resulting in a surge of
attention and influx of capital. Baltas and Kosowski (2013) provide an overview of this phe-
nomenon. By the end of 2011, CTA funds had surpassed 300billionwithinthe2 trillion hedge fund
industry. However, CTA performance was underwhelming, and one possible reason for this is that
the capacity had reached a ceiling.
       Baltas and Kosowski (2013) use two methods to explore whether market capacity affects trend
following strategies. The first method was a regression analysis with fund inflows as the inde-
pendent variable and trend following performance as the dependent variable. The results showed
that the regression coefficient was negative but not statistically significant, indicating that fund
inflows did not affect the performance of trend following models. The second method involved ca-
pacity estimation, assuming the entire AUM of systematic CTAs were invested in trend following
strategies. The results showed that the positions required for most assets did not exceed the total
position size of each asset; in fact, the positions of trend following models represented only a small
fraction of the overall derivatives market. Therefore, the poor performance of trend following was
not significantly related to capacity.


9.2       Increased Asset Correlation
       Georgopoulou and Wang (2016) also investigate the poor performance of trend following in
recent years, attributing the primary reason to a shift in asset correlations. Specifically, after the
financial crisis, central banks worldwide adopted quantitative easing (QE) to stimulate economic

development, which disrupted and distorted the correlation among various asset classes. Unlike
cross-sectional momentum, time-series momentum strategies prefer assets with low correlations.
The increasing correlations led to fewer independent trends, weakening the effectiveness of trend
following strategies.
   To confirm this hypothesis, Georgopoulou and Wang (2016) perform a regression with trend
following returns as the dependent variable and the QE dummy variable as the independent
variable. The results showed that the QE coefficient was significantly negative, indicating that
quantitative easing indeed affects trend following performance.
   Additionally, Baltas (2015) and Baltas and Kosowski (2017) point out that the increase in asset
correlations might be one of the reasons for the poor performance of trend following strategies.
Baltas (2015) calculates the average correlation coefficient using intra-month data, then divided
the assets into four groups based on the strength of their correlations, and evaluated the trend
following performance for each group. The results showed that the higher the correlation, the lower
the returns. Baltas and Kosowski (2017) also find that considering the correlation coefficient helps
improve the performance of trend following strategies.


9.3    Lack of Trends
   AQR’s 2019 report (Babu et al. 2019) provides a detailed analysis of the reasons behind the
failure of trend following strategies. First, they suggested that the lower risk-free rate after the
financial crisis might be one of the factors contributing to the poor performance of trend following
strategies (though not the main cause). This is easy to understand because futures are margin-
based and do not require full capital allocation. Cash management can boost trend following
returns, and a lower risk-free rate naturally reduces this boost.
   Secondly, AQR argued that lack of diversification was not the reason behind the poor per-
formance of trend following. To verify this, AQR defined a Diversification Multiplier, which is
calculated by dividing the Sharpe ratio of the trend following portfolio by the average Sharpe ratio
of individual trend following strategies. The larger this value, the better the diversification effect.
Comparing the diversification multipliers for the full sample (1880–2018) and for 2010 to 2018,
they found minimal difference, indicating that insufficient diversification should not be blamed.
   Finally, AQR pointed the finger at the lack of trends. Their analysis revealed that between
2010 and 2018 the general trend of assets was weak, which was the main reason for the poor
performance of trend following strategies. That said, AQR is not pessimistic about the future of
trend following. If asset trends strengthen in the future, trend following strategies could perform
significantly better.

10      Skepticism
   Although trend following has proven itself over a long period, not everyone is an advocate of
this strategy.
   Kim et al. (2016) point out that time-series momentum only outperforms buy-and-hold and
cross-sectional momentum strategies when volatility adjustments are applied. Without volatility
adjustments, there is no significant difference between time-series momentum and the buy-and-
hold strategy. Furthermore, even with volatility adjustments, the difference between time-series
momentum and buy-and-hold strategies is not significant. They conclude that the reason time-
series momentum is effective is because it involves the use of leverage.
   In addition, Huang et al. (2020) publish a paper titled “Time-series momentum: Is it there?”,
which critically examine Moskowitz et al. (2012). In particular, Huang et al. (2020) study the same
assets as Moskowitz et al. (2012). The authors first tested the predictive power of raw returns of
different assets for future returns and only eight (three) assets had significant predictive ability at
the 10% (5%) significance level. Out-of-sample performance was even worse, with 45 assets having
negative out-of-sample R-squared values. They then replicated the panel regression of Moskowitz
et al. (2012), obtaining similar results, but pointed out that Moskowitz et al. (2012) did not include
an intercept term in their regression, implying an important assumption that assets have the same
Sharpe ratio. However, it is clear that stocks, commodities, foreign exchange, and bonds do not
have the same Sharpe ratio.
   Based on this, Huang et al. (2020) argue that it is necessary to include the average (volatility-
adjusted) returns of different assets in the model to control for the fixed effects; otherwise, the
estimation results would be upwardly biased. The authors applied two bootstrap methods for ad-
justment and found that after controlling for fixed effects, the lagged one-month returns generally
had no significant predictive ability. Although cumulative returns over a period still exhibited
significant predictive power, their significance declined sharply. Huang et al. (2020) further test
out-of-sample performance and find that past returns have significant out-of-sample predictive
power in the stock market, but not in other markets, especially in the bond and foreign exchange
markets, where out-of-sample R-squared values are generally negative.
   Overall, Huang et al. (2020) conclude that past returns do not have significant predictive power,
suggesting that the foundation of time-series momentum, as proposed by Moskowitz et al. (2012),
is not robust. They then proposed the TSH strategy, which does not require return predictions
and determines trade direction based on the asset’s historical average return sign. The test results
reveal that the equal-weighted time-series momentum and TSH strategies do not have significantly
different performances, leading to the conclusion that time-series momentum might be driven by
differences in historical returns between assets. In other words, the excess returns of time-series
momentum are more likely due to the risk premiums earned from holding positions.

11     Conclusion
   Trend following is widely present across various asset classes, and its underlying logic is well-
founded. From a technical standpoint, the most important aspect of a successful trend strategy
is determining the appropriate time scale for the trend. The parameters of the strategy should be
adapted to the return characteristics of the asset. Once the time scale is established, the specific
choice of trend measure does not make a fundamental difference. It might be more effective to
focus efforts on position management to control risk.
   From a risk perspective, trend following is considered a divergent risk-taking strategy. While
it may not be a “holy grail,” its return distribution aligns with the “holy grail distribution,”
which gives it advantages such as crisis alpha, making it highly valuable for portfolio allocation.
However, it is worth noting that studies have shown that excessive use of leverage amplifies both
the returns and risks of such strategies in a nonlinear way. Therefore, sound and effective risk
management is a crucial component of utilizing trend following strategies.
   Although the return distribution of trend following strategies shows positive skewness, from
a psychological perspective, successful implementation of trend following strategies requires a
clear understanding of their risk-return characteristics. The Sharpe ratio of trend strategies on
individual assets is usually not high, but combining trend strategies of different low-correlation
assets can significantly enhance the portfolio’s Sharpe ratio. Even so, trend following strategies
are very likely to experience sustained drawdowns over shorter to medium-term periods (e.g., one
year). Mastering trend following strategies is not about understanding their positive long-term
expectation but rather about comprehending their risk-return characteristics and accepting their
potential underperformance in the short term.
   As an ancient yet highly resilient strategy, trend following strategies warrant our serious study
and research.
