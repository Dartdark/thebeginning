                                                Summary

      Research supports the idea that a mean reversion strategy, particularly with a near-zero beta, can be
successful. These findings were examined by creating and backtesting an algorithm that would trade
autonomously while following a set of instructions with optimized parameters. With the results from the
backtest and live paper trading, it can be concluded that the strategy could plausibly perform well, but some
improvements should be made to make it more robust.




                                  Motivation and Rationale

        The idea of creating an algorithm that would trade on its own and perform well is interesting both
personally and professionally. To that end, after considering potential strategies, a mean reversion strategy
with a beta-zero targeting overlay was chosen as a starting point.

       Market research reports evidence that returns are negatively serially correlated.2 The hypothesis that
stock prices follow a random walk can be rejected1 and it can be shown that there is significant
autocorrelation in long-horizon returns.2 If implemented properly, a mean reversion strategy can be used to
generate alpha - a suggestion supported by Fama and French.

         In addition to mean reversion, a mechanism that would minimize the portfolio’s beta could enhance
performance. Black, Jensen, and Scholes [BJS, 1972] and Miller and Scholes [1972] found that in the period
from 1931 through 1965, low-beta stocks in the United States did better than the capital asset pricing model
predicts, while high-beta stocks did worse. Several authors find that this pattern continued in subsequent
years, at least through 1989. Fama and French extend it through 1990.1 Extending the same idea to portfolios
- that low-beta portfolios will outperform high-beta portfolios over long periods of time - could prove to be
true.

       Quantopian.com, a website that provides historical data and an online development environment, was
used to code the algorithm in Python and run backtests.

                                     Current Methodology

       The algorithm is outlined below:

Mean Reversion
1. After the market closes on Friday, scrub the week’s trading data to find the stocks with the highest dollar
   volume liquidity. In this case, the top 3% of stocks by dollar volume in a one-day lookback.
2. Define the “low return group” as the set of the stocks with the bottom 3.6% of weekly returns among
   those in the high dollar volume group
3. Define the “high return group” as the set of the stocks with the highest 0.4% of weekly returns among
   those in the high dollar volume group
4. Buy the low return group and short the high return group on Monday at noon
Beta-Zero Targeting
5. Buy/short the index (SPY) and/or the 20-year treasury bond (TLT) to target a beta of 0 (relative to SPY),
   based on a one-year lookback

      The variables here - shown in red above - were all datamined to produce the best results in backtests,
so some more thought should be put into their exact values for a more robust solution. Going forward,
perhaps a Monte Carlo analysis or some more research could aid in finding ideal parameters.

                         Next Steps and Analysis Iterations

•   Optimization vs. Robustness

        The values of the algorithm’s current parameters make it somewhat optimized, but its robustness can
        be improved. Its maximum drawdown since it began trading is -10.52%, which is far from ideal. A
        Monte Carlo analysis could be performed to ascertain the ideal parameters for the algorithm.

•   Sector Concentration Considerations

        Because the strategy focuses solely on high dollar volume stocks, highly traded sectors are
        automatically favored. For example, healthcare and pharmaceutical stocks are often shorted the week
        after news of their successful drug trials. A check could be put into place to limit sector concentration
        to lower risk.

•   Potential Role of Human Intervention

        The fundamentals are ignored in purely technical strategies like this one, so mistakes are bound to be
made without human intervention - for example, this strategy went long Equifax the Monday after
their data breach was announced. Using the algorithm as a guide rather than a rulebook is necessary.
A potential solution could involve producing the next week’s proposed trades on Friday after the
market closes and then doing research over the weekend to validate those trades before execution.
