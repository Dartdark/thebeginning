                        The Two Primitives of Spot Market Strategy




                                              Fikri Direnç Aktaş




                                                 Working Paper


                                                   May 2026




Abstract

This paper argues that in a single, reasonably efficient spot market, there exist only two fundamental trading
strategies. The argument rests on a logical constraint: in spot markets, profit derives exclusively from price
movement in the direction of one's position. For a trader to enter a position, they must have a reason to
expect that movement. That reason must take one of two forms: either the current price is low relative to
some reference level and should rise (the logic of Mean Reversion), or the current price is high relative to
some reference level and signals a breakout that should continue (the logic of Momentum). There is no third
positional argument. A price that is neither low nor high—a price in the middle—provides no directional
justification and is untradeable in spot markets. This logical constraint, not merely empirical resemblance,
makes Mean Reversion and Momentum the exhaustive primitives of spot market strategy. We demonstrate
that value investing, growth investing, arbitrage, pairs trading, and trend following all reduce to variations of
these two primitives, and briefly discuss the conditions under which each dominates.

Keywords: momentum, mean reversion, market strategy taxonomy, trend following, value investing,
arbitrage

1. Introduction

In a spot market, there is only one way to profit: buy an asset, wait for its price to rise, and sell it at a higher
price. Whatever the strategy, whatever the philosophy, whatever the analytical framework, the profit
mechanism is always the same. Price must move in the direction of the position.

This simple fact imposes a logical constraint on the number of possible strategies. To enter a position, a
trader must have a reason to expect that the price will rise from its current level. That reason must be a
statement about the current price relative to something else. What kind of statement can it be?

There are only two possibilities.

The first possibility: The current price is low relative to some reference level. It has fallen below its
historical average. It is below its estimated intrinsic value. It is cheap compared to a related asset. The trader
buys because the deviation should correct. The price should rise because it is low. This is the logic of Mean
Reversion.

The second possibility: The current price is high relative to some reference level. It has broken above its
recent trading range. It is above its moving average. Its earnings trajectory is accelerating beyond peers. The
trader buys because the deviation signals strength rather than error. The price should continue rising because
the breakout reveals an anomaly—a trend that has not yet been fully priced. This is the logic of Momentum.

There is no third possibility. A price that is neither low nor high—a price sitting comfortably in the middle of
its range, at fair value, showing no breakout and no deviation—provides no reason to expect movement in
either direction. It is, from the perspective of spot market trading, inert. It offers no trade.

This logical argument, not merely an empirical observation about risk profiles, is why Mean Reversion and
Momentum are the only two fundamental strategies in spot markets. They are not two among many. They
are the only two possible justifications for directional price expectations.

The middle position—the absence of signal—is precisely the gap that structured products fill. Through
derivative contracts, traders can profit from stillness, time decay, and volatility contraction rather than
directional movement. That synthetic third strategy is the subject of related work1.The present paper focuses
on the natural primitives themselves.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews the empirical evidence for momentum and mean
reversion, the theoretical explanations that have been proposed, and the taxonomic gap this paper addresses.
Section 3 develops the logical argument that these two strategies exhaust the possibilities of spot market
trading. Section 4 defines each primitive—its statistical basis, behavioral logic, implementation, and risk
profile—and distinguishes cross-sectional from time-series momentum. Section 5 demonstrates that existing
investment philosophies, from value investing to pairs trading, map cleanly onto the two primitives. Section
6 briefly addresses regime dependence. Section 7 concludes.

2. Literature Review

The two strategies at the center of this paper—Momentum and Mean Reversion—are independently well-
documented in the empirical finance literature. This section reviews the evidence for each, the theoretical
explanations that have been proposed, and the absence of a unified taxonomy that this paper addresses.



2.1 Momentum: Empirical Evidence

The momentum effect—the tendency for assets with high past returns to continue outperforming—was first
systematically documented by Jegadeesh and Titman (1993). Using U.S. equity data from 1965 to 1989,
they found that portfolios buying past six-month winners and selling past six-month losers generated
approximately 1% per month in excess returns. The effect was robust across formation and holding periods
and was not explained by standard risk factors.

Subsequent research confirmed momentum's pervasiveness. Rouwenhorst (1998) documented return
continuation across twelve European markets. Moskowitz, Ooi, and Pedersen (2012) extended the analysis
to time-series momentum—evaluating each asset against its own past rather than ranking assets against
peers—and found significant Sharpe ratios across equity indices, currencies, commodities, and bonds.
Asness, Moskowitz, and Pedersen (2013) demonstrated that momentum profits exist across eight distinct
markets and asset classes, concluding that momentum reflects a common source of variation operating
across the entire financial landscape.



2.2 Mean Reversion: Empirical Evidence

Mean reversion—the tendency for prices to revert to a historical average after extreme deviations—has an
equally long intellectual history. Poterba and Summers (1988) documented long-horizon mean reversion in
U.S. stock prices, finding negative autocorrelation in returns over multi-year horizons. De Bondt and Thaler
(1985, 1987) showed that extreme losers outperform extreme winners over three- to five-year periods—a
contrarian effect opposite to short-term momentum. In the practitioner literature, mean reversion forms the
basis for statistical arbitrage and pairs trading (Gatev, Goetzmann, & Rouwenhorst, 2006).



2.3 Theoretical Explanations

The theoretical literature has developed competing explanations for these phenomena.

Behavioral models attribute momentum to investor underreaction and mean reversion to overreaction.
Barberis, Shleifer, and Vishny (1998) model a representative investor subject to conservatism bias (updating
beliefs too slowly, generating momentum) and representativeness heuristic (extrapolating recent trends too
far, generating subsequent reversals). Hong and Stein (1999) propose a model in which "news watchers"


                                                      2
trade on fundamentals and "momentum traders" extrapolate past returns; gradual information diffusion
creates initial underreaction, which momentum traders subsequently amplify into overreaction and eventual
reversal.

Institutional models offer a structural alternative. Vayanos and Woolley (2013) develop a rational model of
momentum and reversal based on delegated portfolio management. Fund flows respond gradually to changes
in perceived managerial ability, creating price continuation in the short run and reversal in the long run. This
mechanism does not require behavioral biases; it arises from the adjustment costs and incentive structures
facing institutional investors.

Risk-based explanations have been proposed for both phenomena but remain contested. Conrad and Kaul
(1998) argued that momentum profits reflect cross-sectional variation in expected returns, but Jegadeesh and
Titman (2001) found that most momentum profits derive from time-series predictability rather than static
expected return differences. Daniel and Moskowitz (2016) characterized momentum strategies as crash-
prone, but this characterization applies primarily to unmanaged cross-sectional portfolios rather than the
time-series strategies with built-in risk management employed in practice (Covel, 2017).



2.4 The Taxonomic Gap

Despite the richness of these literatures, a unified taxonomy is absent. Momentum and mean reversion are
treated as separate anomalies requiring separate explanations—behavioral, institutional, or risk-based. The
literature does not ask whether these two strategies might exhaust the logical space of spot market trading,
nor whether other strategies might reduce to variations of these two. This paper addresses that gap. It does
not propose new empirical evidence for either strategy. It proposes a logical framework that positions them
as the only two possible strategies in spot markets, and demonstrates that existing investment philosophies
map cleanly onto this framework.



2.5 Related Work

This paper is part of a broader research program. In related work, I extend the two-primitives framework to
structured products, arguing that range-based option strategies constitute a synthetic third strategy designed
to fill the structural gap left by sideways markets where neither primitive functions effectively2. The present
paper provides the foundational taxonomy upon which that extension builds.

3. The Logical Argument: Why Only Two

3.1 The Profit Constraint

In a spot market, profit requires directional price movement. An investor buys an asset. The price must rise
for the investor to realize a gain. There is no alternative mechanism—no time decay to harvest, no volatility
premium to collect, no spread to capture from simultaneous buying and selling in different markets (the
latter constituting arbitrage, which we will show is itself a form of mean reversion).

For non-yielding spot assets, the investor's entire return derives from a single source: price moving in the
desired direction. For yield-bearing assets, price movement remains the primary source of trading returns,
though total return includes income components. Section 3.6 addresses this qualification.

This means that every spot market strategy must answer one question: Why do I expect the price to rise from
here?



3.2 The Positional Constraint

The answer to that question must take the form of a positional statement. The current price must be
characterized relative to something—its past, its peers, its fundamentals, its recent range. Without such a
reference, the statement "price will rise" is groundless.

What kinds of positional statements are logically available?

Every price exists somewhere on a spectrum relative to a chosen reference level. It is either below the
reference, at the reference, or above it. These three positions—low, middle, high—exhaust the logical
possibilities.



3.3 Low: The Logic of Mean Reversion

If the current price is low relative to a reference level—below its historical average, below its intrinsic value,
below a correlated asset—the trader has a reason to buy. The deviation should correct. Prices that are
unusually low tend to rise. This expectation is not arbitrary; it rests on a specific statistical property:
negative autocorrelation. Extreme observations in a stationary process tend to be followed by observations
closer to the mean.

This positional argument—"price will rise because it is low"—is the logical foundation of every mean
reversion strategy, whether implemented with oscillators on price data or with valuation multiples on
fundamental data.




                                                        4
3.4 High: The Logic of Momentum

If the current price is high relative to a reference level—breaking above its recent trading range, crossing
above its moving average, showing earnings growth exceeding peers—the trader also has a reason to buy.
But the logic is different. The price is high not because of random fluctuation that will correct, but because
something has changed. The deviation is an anomaly that signals a new regime. The breakout reveals
information that the market has not yet fully absorbed.
This positional argument—"price will continue rising because it is high and breaking out"—rests on a
different statistical property: positive autocorrelation. In a trending process, above-average observations tend
to be followed by further above-average observations.



3.5 Middle: The Absence of Signal

Now consider the middle position. The price is at its historical average, at fair value, in the middle of its
range, showing no breakout and no deviation. There is no reason—from either logic—to expect movement
in either direction. The price is where it "should" be.

This is not a tradeable condition. The spot market investor has no natural mechanism to profit from a price
that is correctly valued and stationary. The middle is an absence of signal. It requires a synthetic strategy—
derivatives, options, structured products—to monetize.



3.6 A Qualification: Yield-Bearing Assets

The argument above assumes that the sole source of return is price appreciation. This is true for non-yielding
assets—commodities, currencies held as speculation, non-dividend-paying stocks. But many spot market
assets generate yield: stocks pay dividends, bonds pay coupons, and currencies held as investments earn
interest rate differentials.

Does yield create a third primitive?

First, yield in stillness resembles the synthetic third strategy. An investor who buys a dividend-paying
stock and holds it through a flat price environment earns a return from business activity rather than price
movement. This is functionally similar to the structured products discussed in related work (Aktaş, 2026a): a
premium collected from the passage of time rather than from directional accuracy. In this sense, yield-
bearing spot assets partially fill the "middle" gap identified in Section 3.5—they make stillness partially
tradeable without derivatives. However, they do so not through a new trading logic but through a
fundamentally different source of return: the distribution of business profits to owners. This is not
a strategy in the trading sense; it is a claim on cash flows. The dividend investor is not making a positional
argument about price. They are collecting an ownership entitlement.
Second, yield does not create a new positional logic. When a trader makes an active decision to enter a
position—as opposed to passively holding for yield—they must still justify why now. If the justification is


                                                       5
"the dividend is attractive," the implicit argument is that the price has fallen enough to make the yield
competitive relative to alternatives. That is a "low" argument: the price is low relative to the income stream.
It reduces to mean reversion. If the justification is "the company is growing its dividend," the implicit
argument is that the growth trajectory will continue. That is a "high" argument: the payout is breaking out
relative to its history. It reduces to momentum.

Third, yield can dominate price movement without replacing the positional logic. A bond with a high
coupon may generate positive total return even if the price declines modestly. But the decision to buy that
bond still requires a directional view (or at least the view that price decline won't overwhelm the yield). The
yield modifies the risk-reward calculus but does not eliminate the need for a positional justification.

In summary: Yield-bearing assets blur the boundary between natural and synthetic returns—they make
stillness partially profitable without derivatives. But they do not introduce a third positional logic. The active
trading decision still reduces to buying because the price is low (yield is attractive relative to price) or
buying because the breakout is continuing (yield is growing). The two primitives remain exhaustive of
trading strategies, even if the total return calculation includes non-price components.



3.7 The Exhaustion Claim

The argument can be summarized as follows:

   1. Spot market profit requires directional price movement.

   2. Expecting directional movement requires a positional justification.

   3. Positional justifications exhaust in three categories: low, middle, high.

   4. "Low" generates the logic of Mean Reversion (correction expected).

   5. "High" generates the logic of Momentum (continuation expected).

   6. "Middle" provides no directional justification and is untradeable in spot markets.
   7. Therefore, there are exactly two natural spot market strategies.

This is not an empirical claim about what strategies happen to exist or what risk profiles happen to resemble
each other. It is a logical claim about the structure of spot market trading itself. If you accept the premise
that profit requires directional movement and that directional expectations require positional justification,
the conclusion follows necessarily.

4. The Two Primitives Defined

Having established why there are only two primitives, we now define what they are.



4.1 Mean Reversion

Mean reversion is the strategy of buying assets whose prices are low relative to a reference level, based on
the expectation that the deviation will correct. The reference level may take several forms: a historical
moving average (price-based mean reversion), an estimate of intrinsic value (fundamental-based mean
reversion, commonly known as value investing), a contemporaneous price in another market (spatial
arbitrage), or the historical spread relative to a paired asset (relative value or pairs trading). What unifies
these approaches is not the reference level itself but the underlying logic: the price is low, the deviation
should correct, and the correction produces the return.

The statistical foundation of mean reversion is negative autocorrelation. In a stationary process, extreme
observations tend to be followed by observations closer to the mean. This property is not unique to financial
markets—it is a general feature of any process with a stable distribution, from heights in a population to
measurement errors in scientific instruments. In asset prices, Poterba and Summers (1988) documented
long-horizon negative autocorrelation in U.S. equity returns, providing early formal evidence for the
phenomenon.

The behavioral explanation for mean reversion is overreaction. De Bondt and Thaler (1985, 1987) argued
that investors systematically overreact to news, driving prices beyond fundamental justification during both
selloffs and rallies. These dislocations are temporary. As the emotional response fades and fundamental
analysis reasserts itself, prices revert toward prior levels. The mean reversion trader profits by identifying
these overreactions and positioning for their correction.
Common implementations include oscillators such as the Relative Strength Index (Wilder, 1978) and
Stochastic indicators, which measure the speed and magnitude of recent price changes to identify
overbought and oversold conditions. Volatility bands, such as Bollinger Bands (Bollinger, 2001) and Keltner
Channels, place statistically-derived boundaries around a moving average; touches of the lower band signal
potential mean reversion to the upside, and touches of the upper band signal potential reversion to the
downside. Pairs trading (Gatev, Goetzmann, & Rouwenhorst, 2006) extends the logic to relative
relationships between correlated assets.

The characteristic risk profile of mean reversion strategies combines a high win rate with a low win/loss
ratio. The strategy generates frequent small gains as prices revert from temporary extremes. These are
punctuated by occasional large losses when deviations persist—either because the trend is stronger than
anticipated or because the reference level itself has permanently shifted. The practitioner metaphor "picking
up pennies in front of a steamroller" captures this asymmetry: steady, modest profits that can be erased by a
single catastrophic event when the expected reversion fails to materialize.




                                                        7
4.2 Momentum

Momentum is the strategy of buying assets whose prices are high relative to a reference level and breaking
out, based on the expectation that the breakout signals a new trend that will continue. As with mean
reversion, the reference level may vary: a moving average or recent price range (trend following), a peer
group ranking (cross-sectional momentum), or an earnings trajectory (growth investing). What unifies these
approaches is the underlying logic: the price is high, the elevation signals a persistent anomaly rather than a
temporary deviation, and the continuation produces the return.

The statistical foundation of momentum is positive autocorrelation, or autoregression. In a trending process,
above-average values tend to be followed by further above-average values—the opposite of the mean-
reverting pattern. Jegadeesh and Titman (1993) first systematically documented this property in U.S.
equities, finding that portfolios buying past six-month winners and selling past six-month losers generated
approximately one percent per month in excess returns. Moskowitz, Ooi, and Pedersen (2012) extended the
analysis to time-series momentum across equity indices, currencies, commodities, and bonds, demonstrating
that the effect is pervasive across asset classes.

The behavioral explanation for momentum is underreaction. Barberis, Shleifer, and Vishny (1998) modeled
investors who update their beliefs too slowly in response to new information, generating gradual price drift
in the direction of the news. Hong and Stein (1999) proposed a complementary mechanism in which "news
watchers" trade on fundamental information while "momentum traders" extrapolate past returns; the gradual
diffusion of information among news watchers creates initial underreaction, which momentum traders
subsequently amplify. A structural alternative emphasizes institutional constraints: Vayanos and Woolley
(2013) demonstrated that large funds executing trades gradually to minimize market impact generate price
patterns empirically indistinguishable from behavioral underreaction, but arising from rational execution
strategy rather than cognitive bias.

Common implementations include moving average crossovers, which generate buy signals when a shorter-
term average crosses above a longer-term average, and channel breakouts such as Donchian channels, which
generate buy signals when prices exceed the highest level of a specified lookback period. Relative strength
rankings, the basis for cross-sectional momentum, compare assets against peers and select the strongest
performers.

The characteristic risk profile of momentum strategies combines a low win rate with a high win/loss ratio—
the mirror image of mean reversion. The strategy generates frequent small losses during sideways or choppy
markets when breakouts fail and trends do not establish. These are punctuated by occasional large gains
during sustained directional movements. The practitioner discipline of "cutting losses short and letting
winners run" (Covel, 2017) reflects this profile: the strategy accepts many small losses as the cost of being
positioned for the infrequent large trends that generate the bulk of returns.



4.3 Two Variants of Momentum

The momentum literature distinguishes two implementations that, while sharing the same underlying logic,
exhibit meaningfully different risk characteristics.



                                                       8
Cross-sectional momentum ranks assets against each other based on past returns over a formation period,
typically six to twelve months. The highest-performing assets (winners) are bought, and the lowest-
performing assets (losers) are sold short. This is a relative strength approach: performance is measured
against peers, not against an asset's own history. The strategy requires short-selling capability and, as Daniel
and Moskowitz (2016) documented, is exposed to severe losses during market rebounds when heavily
shorted loser stocks experience dramatic reversals. These "momentum crashes" are a well-known
vulnerability of the cross-sectional implementation.

Time-series momentum, also called trend following, evaluates each asset independently against its own
past performance. Long positions are taken in assets with positive recent returns; positions are exited or
reversed when returns turn negative. This approach, formalized academically by Moskowitz, Ooi, and
Pedersen (2012) but practiced by commodity trading advisors for decades, does not require short selling—
the strategy can simply exit to cash when trends reverse. The built-in risk management through trend-
reversal signals produces a fundamentally different risk profile from unmanaged cross-sectional portfolios:
vulnerability to whipsaws during sideways markets rather than vulnerability to catastrophic crashes during
market rebounds. The distinction is not merely academic; it has significant practical implications for
portfolio construction and risk management.

5. The Reductionist Demonstration

If the logical argument of Section 3 is correct, then every existing spot market strategy must reduce to either
Mean Reversion or Momentum. This section demonstrates that mapping. Importantly, the reduction is not
based on resemblance of risk profiles or economic outcomes. It is based on the positional logic each strategy
employs.



5.1 Value Investing as Mean Reversion

Value investing, as articulated by Graham and Dodd (1934) and practiced by investors such as Warren
Buffett, involves buying securities that trade below their estimated intrinsic value and waiting for market
prices to converge to that value. The positional logic is straightforward: the price is low relative to intrinsic
value, and that deviation should correct.

The information set is fundamental rather than price-based. Value investors analyze earnings, book value,
discounted cash flows, and competitive position to estimate what a company is worth. The reference level—
intrinsic value—is derived from these fundamentals rather than from historical price data. But the
underlying logic is identical to price-based mean reversion. A deviation is identified (price below intrinsic
value), a correction is expected (price rising to meet value), and the correction produces the return.

The risk profile is also analogous. Value investors win regularly when the market recognizes the discrepancy
and prices converge. But they can suffer extended drawdowns when cheap stocks become cheaper—the
"value trap," which is the fundamental equivalent of catching a falling knife in price-based mean reversion.
In both cases, the strategy fails when the reference level shifts or when the deviation persists longer than the
investor's patience or capital.



5.2 Growth Investing as Momentum

Growth investing involves buying companies with strong and accelerating earnings growth, expecting that
growth trajectory to continue and to drive prices higher. The positional logic mirrors momentum: the
earnings trajectory is high relative to peers or history and is breaking out. That breakout signals a persistent
anomaly—something about this company's competitive position, market opportunity, or execution capability
is different—and the anomaly should continue.

The information set is forward-looking fundamentals: earnings growth rates, revenue expansion, market
share gains, and total addressable market. The reference level is the growth trajectory of peers or the
company's own historical growth rate. But the underlying logic is identical to price-based momentum. A
trend is identified (earnings growth), continuation is expected (growth will persist, and prices will follow),
and that continuation produces the return.
The risk profile is similarly analogous. Growth investors suffer frequent small losses on companies whose
growth proves unsustainable or already priced in, punctuated by occasional massive gains on the rare


                                                        10
companies that sustain exceptional trajectories over many years. This is precisely the "many small losses,
occasional huge gains" profile of price-based momentum.



5.3 Arbitrage as Spatial Mean Reversion

Arbitrage, in its purest form, involves buying an asset in one market where it is undervalued and
simultaneously selling an identical asset in another market where it is overvalued. The positional logic is
mean reversion applied across space rather than time: the price is low in one market relative to another
market for the same asset, and that deviation should correct.
The information set consists of cross-market prices. The reference level is the contemporaneous price in the
other market. The arbitrageur does not need to estimate intrinsic value or analyze historical price patterns—
the reference is directly observable in real time. But the underlying logic remains mean reversion. A
deviation is identified (price discrepancy between markets), convergence is expected (the law of one price),
and the convergence produces the return.

The primary risk—that the gap widens before it closes—is the spatial analogue of the temporal risk faced by
price-based mean reversion traders. Just as a stock can become cheaper before it reverts, an arbitrage spread
can widen before it converges. The structural vulnerability is identical; only the dimension (space versus
time) differs.



5.4 Pairs Trading as Relative Mean Reversion

Pairs trading identifies two historically correlated assets, monitors their price ratio or spread, and enters
positions when that relationship diverges beyond a statistical threshold. The positional logic is mean
reversion applied to a relative relationship rather than an absolute price level: one asset's price is low relative
to a historically correlated asset, and that relative deviation should correct.

The information set consists of cross-asset price ratios. The reference level is the historical spread or ratio
between the two assets, typically estimated using cointegration or correlation analysis over a calibration
period. As with arbitrage, the reference is empirically derived rather than fundamentally estimated. But the
logic remains mean reversion: a deviation from a stable relationship is expected to reverse.

The characteristic risk is that the historical correlation breaks down—the two assets decouple permanently
due to structural changes in one or both. This is the relational analogue of the mean reversion trader's risk
that the reference level has permanently shifted. In both cases, the strategy fails when the assumption of
stationarity is violated.




                                                        11
5.5 Trend Following and Cross-Sectional Momentum as Momentum

Trend following and cross-sectional momentum are the purest expressions of the momentum primitive. Both
use price-based information sets and price-based reference levels. The positional logic is identical: the price
is high relative to a reference and breaking out, and that breakout signals a persistent anomaly.

In trend following, the reference is the asset's own history—its moving average or recent trading range. The
price is high relative to where it has been, and the breakout suggests that the trend will continue. In cross-
sectional momentum, the reference is the asset's peer group. The price is high relative to competitors, and
that relative strength suggests continued outperformance.

No further reduction is needed. These strategies are the primitive itself, expressed directly in price data
without fundamental intermediation.

6. Regime Dependence

The logical argument establishes that there are only two primitives. It does not establish which one will
work at a given time. That depends on market structure.

Momentum requires trending markets. Without sustained directional movement, breakouts fail and the
strategy is whipsawed—accumulating small losses that erode capital.

Mean reversion requires range-bound markets. Without identifiable boundaries around a stable reference
level, deviations may not correct. The strategy either is starved of entry signals or suffers catastrophic losses
when the reference level has permanently shifted.

Strategy selection is therefore regime diagnosis. The investor's first task is not choosing between value and
growth, or between RSI and moving average crossovers. It is determining whether the current market
structure rewards the logic of correction or the logic of continuation. For an empirical demonstration of
regime-dependent strategy performance—specifically, the dominance of momentum over mean reversion in
an institution-dominated, inflation-driven emerging market—see Aktaş (2026b). What determines which
regime prevails is the subject of related work (Aktaş, 2026c).

7. Conclusion

This paper has argued that spot market strategy is constrained by a simple logical structure. Profit requires
directional price movement. Directional expectations require positional justification—a statement about
whether the current price is low, high, or in the middle relative to some reference. "Low" generates the logic
of Mean Reversion. "High" generates the logic of Momentum. "Middle" generates no tradeable signal in
spot markets and requires synthetic strategies to monetize.

This logical argument is stronger than an empirical claim about resemblance. It does not merely observe that
value investing looks like mean reversion. It demonstrates that value investing cannot be anything other
than mean reversion, because the only way to justify buying after a decline is the expectation of correction.
Similarly, growth investing cannot be anything other than momentum, because the only way to justify
buying a company whose metrics are already elevated is the expectation that the elevation signals a
persistent anomaly rather than a temporary deviation.

The two primitives are mirror images. They rest on opposite statistical properties. They generate opposite
risk profiles. They require opposite market structures. And together, they exhaust the logical space of spot
market strategy.
The framework presented here serves as a foundation. A complete strategy space also requires synthetic
strategies—derivatives-based constructions that profit from stillness rather than movement (Aktaş, 2026a).
And a complete investment process requires understanding why a given regime prevails—the political
economy of market structure (Aktaş, 2026c). But the starting point is here: in a spot market, you can buy
because the price is low, or you can buy because the price is high and breaking out. There is no third reason.
