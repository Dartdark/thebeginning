                                                           Abstract
          The rapid growth of exchange-traded funds (ETFs) has raised important questions about the role of
      ETF flows in the pricing and performance of underlying equities. Using the transparency of the ETF
      structure, we examine the relationship between past ETF flows and subsequent stock returns. While
      short-term mean reversion effects are well documented, evidence on longer-term effects remains limited.
         Based on a broad dataset covering a substantial share of the global ETF market capitalization, we find
      that flows from thematic and sectoral ETFs are associated with distinct return patterns across investment
      horizons, with short-term reversal effects and longer-term trend-following dynamics particularly visible
      in U.S. equities.
         Building on these findings, we construct a systematic cross-sectional equity signal that combines short-
      and long-horizon ETF flow information. The resulting portfolios exhibit meaningful return dispersion and
      low correlation with traditional equity factors, suggesting that ETF flows may provide a complementary
      source of information for stock selection and systematic portfolio construction. The effects are weaker in
      European equities, where ETF ownership remains lower.

    Keywords– ETF, ETF flows, ETF ownership, factor investing, smart beta, cross-sectional stock returns, thematic
investing, portfolio construction

1       Introduction
    As many segments of the market seem to become more flow-driven than fundamentally driven, an expanding
strand of the academic literature has focused on the impact of fund flows on underlying equity markets. Whether in
the context of mutual funds or ETFs, this issue has been a key concern for researchers for years.
     Prior research has examined the impact of mutual fund flows on the returns of underlying securities. Warther
(1995) is among the first to examine the relationship between mutual fund flows and subsequent security returns.
He studies this link across equities, bonds, and gold, and finds that unusually large inflows are contemporaneously
associated with higher security returns. However, he reports little evidence that expected flows have predictive power
for future returns. Coval and Stafford (2007) also document that large capital outflows force mutual funds to liquidate
commonly held positions, generating significant downward price pressure, so-called “fire sales”, while large inflows
similarly induce price pressure through coordinated purchases. Their evidence suggests that flow-induced trading can
temporarily push stock prices away from fundamentals, even in highly liquid equity markets. Beyond short-term price
pressure, the authors provide evidence of a longer-term trend-following dynamic. When inflow-induced purchases are
broad relative to available sellers, coordinated institutional trading creates persistent price distortions that endure
for several months, typically around two quarters, and dissipate only slowly over subsequent quarters.
    With the growing importance of the ETF market, several authors have extended similar analyses specifically to
ETFs. Global ETF assets under management reached an all-time high of $18 trillion in 20251 , following a prolonged
period of exponential growth. Over the past decade, investors have increasingly adopted the ETF vehicle, attracted
by its transparency, tax efficiency, cost efficiency, and intraday trading. The ETF market has evolved substantially
and now spans virtually all major asset classes. It enables investors to access far more than traditional market
benchmarks, offering exposure to factor strategies, thematic investments, and quantitative approaches, among others.
More recently, the expansion of ETF assets under management has been driven in part by the development of active
ETFs, which blend active portfolio management with the liquidity, transparency, and operational efficiency of the
ETF structure. This fast-growing segment has outpaced the broader ETF market and encouraged new entrants to
launch products within the ETF ecosystem.
    From an academic perspective, the effect of ETF flows on the underlying equity market is of particular interest.
Owing to their high level of transparency, ETFs provide granular data that facilitate empirical investigation. Fur-
thermore, in contrast to mutual fund flows which may be partially absorbed through cash holdings, ETF flows
mechanically result in proportional investment and divestment across the entire underlying portfolio.
    As highlighted by Ben-David, Franzoni and Moussawi (2017), ETFs can have two opposing effects on stock prices.
The additional liquidity they provide can enhance price discovery in the underlying securities, making prices more
informative. At the same time, ETF trading that is not driven by fundamentals may spill over to the underlying
securities, and potentially leading to mispricing. The authors suggest that these two opposing effects can occur
simultaneously.
    Several studies provide evidence that ETF flows generate non-fundamental price effects on underlying securities.
Da and Shive (2018) show that ETF arbitrageurs act as a transmission channel for non-fundamental shocks to the
equity market. As a result, stocks included in ETFs display stronger comovement, driven by investor reactions to
index-related information rather than firm-specific news. Prior research also documents that ETF-induced trading
activity can create temporary price pressure at the underlying securities level, which is followed by price reversals.
Using an ETF flow definition that differs from ours, Staer (2017) finds evidence of a price reversal effect that fades
after a few days. Likewise, Sandberg (2018) develops a model of ETF-induced price impact and documents a modest
and transitory effect on underlying securities. The author further demonstrates that incorporating an ETF flow
sensitivity factor into portfolio construction improves risk-adjusted performance.
    While the literature has extensively documented short-term, non-fundamental price effects of ETF flows, evidence
on their role in reinforcing longer-term market trends remains limited. The Deutsche Bundesbank, in its October 2018
Monthly Report (Deutsche Bundesbank 2018), warned that ETFs could amplify market movements during periods
of correction. The report highlights that procyclicality in ETF investing can amplify existing market trends, as
passive portfolio rebalancing may trigger mechanical selling of underlying securities following price declines, thereby
reinforcing market movements and potentially increasing systemic risk during periods of stress.
    Van der Beck, Bouchaud and Villamaina (2024) document that investors struggle to distinguish between flow-
driven and fundamental returns, thereby reinforcing self-inflated returns through performance-chasing behaviour.
The authors introduce the concept of Ponzi funds, showing that some investors are effectively chasing their own price
impact. Studies pointing to increased comovement are also consistent with a trend-following mechanism.
    This paper makes several contributions to the literature. First, it documents a short-horizon reversal pattern
associated with ETF flows. Second, it shows that the predictive content of ETF flows differs materially across ETF
types: longer-horizon positive return predictability is concentrated in flows from thematic and sectoral ETFs, while it
is not robustly observed for the broader ETF universe. Third, it documents systematic behavioural patterns among
ETF investors. Finally, it demonstrates that the information embedded in ETF flows can be exploited to construct
economically meaningful signals for equity portfolio managers.
    Most existing studies rely on substantially less comprehensive datasets and focus primarily on ETFs listed in the
United States. To our knowledge, this study is the first to use a dataset of this breadth and to analyse ETF flows
across a global universe of ETFs. While most studies also omit data on delisted ETFs, we were able to incorporate
them into our analysis, thereby limiting survivorship bias.

2.2     Methodology
2.2.1    Classification of ETFs
    Following Ben-David, Franzoni, Kim and Moussawi (2022), we classify ETFs according to their investment ob-
jectives, and distinguish four categories :
   1. Thematic ETFs, as defined by the Trackinsight classification,
   2. Sectoral ETFs, which track one or multiple sectors,
   3. Smart beta ETFs, as identified by Trackinsight,
   4. Other ETFs.
    We adopt this categorisation to differentiate between market-access ETFs and specialised ETFs. The rationale is
that flows into specialised ETFs may carry stronger informational content, as they more directly capture investors’
underlying preferences. It is also worth noting that there is no overlap between sectoral and thematic ETFs in our
methodology. We adopt this classification based on the intuition that strong trend-following behaviour can emerge in
ETF markets, as observed during episodes such as the technology rally in 2024 or the defence sector boom in 2025.
To assess this effect, we compute a correlogram based on Spearman rank correlations, which clearly highlights the
presence of trend-following dynamics. Specifically, we examine the relationship between ETF flows into individual
stocks over horizons ranging from 1 to 12 months and their subsequent monthly returns.

ETFs. We define ETF-induced flows for a given stock as :

2.2.2    Model
   We formally examine the relationship suggested by the correlograms between past cumulative ETF pressure and
subsequent stock returns, with the aim of providing rigorous statistical evidence to validate the observed patterns
and assess their relevance for empirical research and practical applications.
    The correlation analysis indicates that flow variations associated with thematic and sectoral ETFs contain sub-
stantially more informational content, whereas flows from other ETF categories exhibit weaker and less systematic
relationships with returns. Accordingly, the subsequent empirical analysis concentrates on these two specifications,
as the regression framework incorporates pressure features constructed either from the full universe of ETFs or
exclusively from the thematic and sectoral ones, allowing for a direct comparison of their relative explanatory power.
    Rather than including all accumulation horizons simultaneously in a single regression, we estimate a separate
regression for each horizon. This approach avoids introducing highly correlated explanatory variables arising from
overlapping flow-accumulation windows and allows the effect associated with each horizon to be assessed independ-
ently. Consistent with the correlogram analysis, this approach allows us to study the relationship between returns
and cumulative flows at each horizon individually, with the objective of isolating and quantifying the statistical
contribution of each phenomenon separately, improving overall interpretability.
   Let ri,[t, t+m] denote the return of stock i during the month following date t, and let ET F P ressure i, [t−n·m, t]
denote the cumulative ETF pressure on stock i during the n months preceding the date t :

modelling raw stock returns, we focus on returns net of broad market fluctuations. The coefficient γ captures the
contribution of market-wide movements, so that the estimation of β relies on variation in stock returns beyond the
market component. Empirically, omitting this control leads to lower statistical precision, indicating that accounting
for market dynamics materially reduces noise. Overall, this specification allows us to estimate the cross-sectional
effect associated with ETF-induced pressure across stocks.

3.2     Temporal Structure and Oscillatory Patterns in Flow Effects
    Correlograms and horizon-by-horizon regressions indicate that the relationship between ETF-induced flows and
subsequent stock market returns is neither monotonic nor independent from one aggregation window to another,
but rather oscillates between opposing phenomena. This dual structure is most clearly visible when ETF-induced
pressure is constructed using thematic and sector-specific ETFs, and we therefore use the same pressure measure in
the sinusoidal specification below.

sinusoidal function of the time lag n, and we weight lagged ETF pressures accordingly. The resulting empirical
profile then resembles an oscillatory adjustment rather than a linear propagation of effects.

3.3     An Application to Equity Portfolio Management
3.3.1   Simulation of historical performances
    Our statistical analysis indicates that ETF flows contain measurable information about subsequent stock returns
within the S&P 500 universe. This finding has material implications for equity portfolio managers operating in this
market. Accordingly, we conduct a backtest of our proposed signal to evaluate its economic relevance and practical
usefulness from a portfolio management perspective.
    We combine the two effects identified in the statistical analysis to exploit both patterns observed in the data. The
long portfolio targets stocks that have experienced substantial ETF inflows over the past year but limited inflows
over the most recent three months, while the short portfolio focuses on the opposite configuration.
    In accordance with the results of the statistical analysis, the signal is constructed exclusively from flows into
thematic and sectoral ETFs. We implement a long–short equity strategy using a portfolio construction approach
similar to that employed in the development of Fama–French factors. To mitigate potential backtest overfitting,
we retain a twelve-month window to capture the long-term pressure effect component and a three-month window
to capture the short-term component, although alternative horizons (11 and 2 months) produce slightly stronger
statistical results.

3.4    Our signal in the Fama-French Framework
    Because our strategy may resemble an ETF-flow-based momentum signal, there is a risk that it simply rep-
resents an alternative construction of the traditional momentum factor. We therefore evaluate our signal within
the Fama–French asset pricing framework to determine whether it delivers incremental explanatory power beyond
established risk factors.
   The Fama–French five-factor model (Fama and French 2015) extends both the CAPM and the earlier three-factor
model to better explain cross-sectional differences in stock returns. It posits that expected returns are driven by
exposure to five systematic risk factors:
   • MKT (Market factor) : the excess return of the market portfolio over the risk-free rate.
   • SMB (Small Minus Big) : the size premium, capturing the historical outperformance of small-cap stocks relative
     to large-cap stocks.
   • HML (High Minus Low) : the value premium, reflecting the tendency of high book-to-market (“value”) firms
     to outperform low book-to-market (“growth”) firms.
   • RMW (Robust Minus Weak) : the profitability factor, indicating that firms with strong operating profitability
     tend to earn higher returns than less profitable firms.
   • CMA (Conservative Minus Aggressive) : the investment factor, showing that firms that invest conservatively
     tend to outperform those that invest aggressively.
    The model’s core insight is that differences in average returns across stocks can largely be explained by their
sensitivities (betas) to these five common risk factors, which proxy for fundamental dimensions of systematic risk
related to size, valuation, profitability, and investment behaviour.
    We augment our analysis by incorporating the momentum factor introduced by (Carhart 1997). Carhart proposes
the WML (Winners Minus Losers) factor to capture return persistence documented in mutual fund performance.
We include this factor alongside the Fama–French five-factor framework, as it is conceptually related to our ETF
flow–based signal and allows us to assess whether our results simply reflect conventional momentum effects.
   The analysis presented in this section relies on Fama–French five-factor model data retrieved from Kenneth
French’s data library 1 .
    We define ITF (Intertemporal Flow Factor) as the long-short portfolio constructed in 3.3. The portfolio goes
long stocks that receive strong inflows from thematic and sectoral ETFs over a 12-month horizon but relatively weak
inflows over the most recent three months. It goes short stocks exhibiting the opposite pattern : low 12-month inflows
and high recent 3-month inflows.
    The ITF portfolio displays low correlations with the Fama–French five factors, supporting the view that it rep-
resents an independent source of systematic return variation in the S&P 500. Notably, its correlation with the
momentum factor (WML) remains modest, indicating that the strategy is not merely a repackaging of traditional
momentum.

3.5    The case of the European market
    ETF ownership data and the correlogram analysis indicate that the influence of the ETF market on the European
equity market appears to be more limited than in the United States. To confirm this intuition, we replicated the
empirical framework on European equities. Specifically, we apply the regression specification detailed in Equation 6
to the constituents of the STOXX 600 index, using ETF-induced pressure measures constructed from thematic
and sectoral ETFs. As in the U.S. analysis, we estimate Equation 6 separately for each of the twelve accumulation
horizons, avoiding the simultaneous inclusion of highly correlated ETF-pressure measures constructed over overlapping
windows.
    A key structural difference between the U.S. and European markets concerns the relative importance of ETFs in
the ownership structure. Whereas ETFs hold approximately 16% of U.S. large-cap equities, their ownership share
in European large-cap stocks is closer to 6%. This substantial difference may affect the strength of the statistical
relationship between ETF-induced flows and subsequent stock returns. In addition, the diffusion of ETFs occurred
later in Europe than in the United States, potentially limiting the persistence and informational content of ETF flows
over the sample period. Taken together, these institutional differences suggest that weaker statistical associations
may be expected in the European setting.
   Table 10 reports the estimation results across cumulative flow windows ranging from one to twelve months.
    The results reveal a clear short-term effect but no statistically robust long-term relationship. At short horizons,
coefficients are strongly negative and statistically significant, particularly over two and three month windows with
significances at the 1% and 5% levels. This provides clear evidence of a mean reversion effect consistent with temporary
price pressure induced by ETF flows. The effect remains detectable, though weaker, up to horizons of around six to
seven months.
    In contrast, no meaningful long-term effect can be identified. Beyond eight months, estimated coefficients are
small and statistically insignificant, with confidence intervals systematically spanning both sides of zero. Unlike the
U.S. case, there is no contiguous range of horizons over which coefficients are positive and significant, preventing any
reliable identification of a long-horizon allocation effect.

4     Discussion
    While the empirical results highlight a clear relation between ETF flows and subsequent equity returns, several
limitations should be acknowledged.
     First, although our dataset covers a large number of ETFs, it does not include the entirety of global ETF assets
under management. Measurement error in ETF ownership and in the construction of stock-level pressure indicators
may therefore attenuate the documented relationships. In addition, the identification of thematic and sectoral ETFs
relies on the classification framework provided by Trackinsight. Alternative classification schemes could reassign
certain products across categories and potentially affect the magnitude and the horizons of the estimated effects.
    Second, the empirical specification relies on fixed horizons to capture short- and longer-term flow dynamics. In
our portfolio application, we use twelve-month and three-month windows for simplicity and transparency. However,
other horizon choices may also be informative. Future work could explore more flexible specifications, including
weighted combinations of horizons guided by the regression results.
    Our portfolio implementation is intentionally simple. Portfolios are equally weighted and constructed purely from
the cross-sectional ranking of the signal in order to isolate the informational content of ETF-induced pressure. In
practical investment settings, alternative constructions may be preferable. Portfolio weights could incorporate market



                                                             17
capitalisation, liquidity characteristics, or signal intensity, while additional constraints such as sector allocation limits
or turnover buffers could be introduced to reduce trading costs.
    Another natural extension concerns the role of mutual funds. Although ETF flows into physically replicated
ETFs are transparent and mechanically transmitted to underlying markets, mutual funds still represent a large share
of delegated equity ownership. Comparing ETF and mutual fund induced pressures would help determine whether
the documented horizon-dependent structure reflects a feature specific to ETF mechanics or a broader property of
flow-driven markets.
    Finally, our results suggest that the strength of ETF flow effects may depend on market structure. The clearer
evidence observed in U.S. equities is consistent with the higher level of ETF penetration and the greater integration
of ETFs into equity trading in that market. Examining how ETF ownership affects cross-sectional price dynamics
across markets remains a possible direction for future research.
    Another avenue for future research would be to develop a time-series ETF flow–based signal, rather than a cross-
sectional one, thereby enabling the identification of unusual flows at the individual firm level. Practitioners may also
be interested in ETF-based signals. In this context, statistical analysis and signal construction could be extended to
thematic and sectoral ETFs to design trend-following strategies for ETF investors.


5     Conclusion
    This paper studies the relation between ETF flows and underlying equity returns within the S&P 500 universe.
Our empirical analysis shows that ETF-induced flows contain information about subsequent stock returns, with effects
that vary across investment horizons. Short-term inflows tend to be followed by partial price reversals, whereas more
sustained inflows from thematic and sectoral ETFs are associated with persistent return effects. These horizon-
dependent dynamics are further supported by a sinusoidal specification linking past flows to subsequent returns. The
short-term reversal effect is consistent with prior studies, although it operates over a slightly longer horizon in our
setting, while the longer-term pattern suggests that ETF flows tend to reinforce prevailing market trends.
    Our findings are particularly pronounced for thematic and sectoral ETFs. Flows originating from these products
appear more informative in highlighting the relationship between flows and returns, likely reflecting tactical investor
positioning and concentrated capital reallocation across segments of the equity market.
    From a portfolio management perspective, ETF flow data therefore provide a useful basis for comparing stocks. In
particular, the signal helps identify securities with particularly high exposure to ETF-induced pressure alongside their
fundamental drivers, and whether this flow-induced pressure is currently supporting or weighing on their performance.
Signals derived from ETF-induced pressure may therefore help equity investors identify stocks supported by persistent
capital inflows, detect securities exposed to short-term flow-driven reversals, and distinguish between positive and
negative flow effects. Our backtests indicate that the long portfolio outperforms the S&P 500 Equally Weighted
benchmark, while the short portfolio materially underperforms, suggesting that the signal may be valuable both for
stock selection and for identifying stocks to avoid.
   Finally, a comparison between the ETF flow–based factor and traditional risk premia indicates limited correlation
with the Fama–French factors and with momentum (WML). This suggests that ETF flow dynamics capture a distinct
dimension of equity market behaviour that may complement existing factor-based allocation frameworks and provide
economically meaningful information for practitioners. Augmenting the Fama–French model by incorporating our
ITF factor shows that the ITF factor is significant for 36% of S&P 500 firms at the 10% level. Moreover, the adjusted
R2 increases by more than one percentage point for firms where the factor is highly significant. These results indicate
that our factor enhances the explanatory power of return variation.
