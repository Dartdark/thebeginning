                                        Abstract

In this paper, we study the relation among market structure, trading costs, and
competition in NASDAQ.         In particular, we address the following questions: Do
NASDAQ dealers exercise market power and extract economic rents in setting bid-ask
spread? How persistent is the market power of dominant dealers? Our estimate of the
rent is approximately 8.76¢, or 0.54% of stock price. The half-life of the persistence of
this rent is approximately 20 months for the entire sample, while the half-life of younger
stocks tend to be shorter than those of more mature stocks. Our result supports Schultz
(2002): NASDAQ dealers make markets only for stocks where they have competitive
advantages in accessing order flow and in information. It might take a while before a
market maker poses effective competition to existing dominant market makers. In the
meantime, incumbent market makers are able to exercise market power and appear to
earn abnormally large profits.

1. Introduction
An essential feature of a dealer market like NASDAQ is competition for order flow
among market makers. The competition presumably helps to ensure that investors get
the best possible quotes and trade prices. Whether or not NASDAQ actually obtains
competitive pricing is an empirical question. One view on this issue is that NASDAQ
operates as a competitive market given a large number of competing market makers and
fluid entry and exit.    Wahal (1997), for instance, finds that entry and exit affect
NASDAQ bid-ask spreads even after controlling for usual explanatory variables. Based
on this finding, Wahal concludes that NASDAQ operates as a competitive market.
However, other recent studies such as Ellis, Michaely and O’Hara (2000, 2002), and
Schultz (2002) suggest that dominant NASDAQ market makers appear to exercise
market power in setting bid-ask prices. Their finding implies that trading costs are
higher than those in a competitive market, and that NASDAQ dealers benefit from
higher trading costs.
       In view of the far-reaching implications of their result, we feel it is important to
take a deeper look at the relation between market structure and trading costs, and
competition.   In this paper, we examine the extent and dynamics of market power of
NASDAQ market makers. In particular, we focus on two questions: (1) Do NASDAQ
market makers enjoy statistically and economically significant market power in setting
bid-ask prices? (2) If yes, then how persistent is the market power? We first establish
the presence (or absence) of market power based on extensive dealer trading volume
data over almost an eight-year period ending December 2000. Previous studies are
based on selected sample over a few months. Focusing on a short period might obscure
trends in economic rents especially if they tend to dissipate over longer time intervals.
We next quantify the economic significance of rent that dominant market makers
extract. Previous studies only look at statistical significance of rent, and have generally
ignored the economic significance of dealer rents. Finally, we estimate the persistence
of dealer rents.   While the magnitude of rent is of concern to both investors and
regulators, its persistence is also an importance issue. If competition works its way to
reduce the rent rapidly, then market power is merely a transitory event. In this case,



                                           1
there would be little need for policy intervention, even when rent is economically
significant.
         Our data include monthly share trading volume by every market maker for every
stock traded on the NASDAQ National Market (NNM) during the period May 1995 to
December 2000. This data set allows us to have an in-depth look at the time series
patterns in competition among market makers. We find that concentration in trading
affects bid-ask spreads even after controlling for the usual variables that explain bid-ask
spreads.    Our measure of market power or “rent” is statistically and economically
significant: rent amounts to 8.76 cents or 0.54% of stock price. We classify stocks by
dollar trading volume: the top 200 stocks (in trading volume) are classified as $-trading
volume group 3, the next 800 stocks as group 2, the rest as group 1.          The average
market maker rent for those groups are approximately identical.         What is different
among these groups is the fraction of rent in stock price. Since stock prices and dollar
trading volume are positively correlated, actively traded stocks tend to have a lower
percentage of rent in price. The average percentage rent is 0.22% for group 3, 0.32%
for group 2 and 0.58% for group 1. Half-life of rent is 20.3 months for entire sample,
and 14.4 months for those stocks which undertook initial public offering during 1995-
1997. Average half-lives of stocks in dollar trading volume groups are approximately
equal.
         This paper is closely related to a growing literature examining the behavior of
dealers in dealer markets. Using extensive entry and exit data for 5,569 NASDAQ
National Market stocks during a period from 1982 to 1993, Wahal (1997) finds that
entry and exit affect bid-ask spreads even after controlling for usual explanatory
variables. This effect is present even among stocks with a large number of market
makers: entry is associated with a 6 cents decrease in end-of-day quoted spread for
stocks with 2-7 market makers, and 2 cents decrease for stocks with more than 20
market makers. Based on this finding, Wahal concludes that NASDAQ operates as a
competitive market.
         Ellis, Michaely and O’Hara (2002) analyze market making activities for the first
seven months for 313 NASDAQ stocks, which went public during September 1996 and
July 1997. They find that the market for market making is highly concentrated with top



                                           2
three dealers handling approximately 75% of trading volume. They observe positive
relation between concentration of trading and bid-ask spreads:          the more trading is
concentrated, the higher the spread, given that all other things are constant. Another
interesting finding is that market quickly reaches to a permanent equilibrium:          “By
virtually every measure of market structure, the market that exists after the first three
months of trading is the market that will characterize trading from then on.”           We
analyze dealers’ behavior as firms reach their adolescent and more mature stages, and
thus complement Ellis, Michaely and O’Hara (2002), which focus on very young firms
during the first 7 months following their initial public offerings.
       Klock and McCormick (1999), among others, document impact upon bid-ask
spreads of the number of market makers and degree of concentration of trading among
market makers. Klock and McCormick (1999) use Herfindahl index as measure of
concentration in market making, and examine relation between Herfindahl index and
bid-ask spreads for NASDAQ stocks for December 1996. Using dealer trading volume
data for all NASDAQ stocks (on both NNM and Small Stock Market) for a period from
May 1995 to February 1998, Schultz (2002) finds that dealers make markets only in
stocks in which they have competitive advantage in accessing order flow and in
information. He notes that those dealers with competitive advantages may appear to
earn abnormally large returns, at least in the short-run. Our results are consistent with
Schultz’ intuition. Market makers indeed appear to earn non-trivial rent with half-life of
14.4 months for IPO stocks.
       Using data for 180 days of trading surrounding implementation of new rules for
88 NASDAQ stocks, Weston (2000) examines competitive structure of the NASDAQ in
the wake of new order handling rules in early 1997. Comparing components of bid-ask
spreads, he finds that order processing components and market maker rent over all
trades have fallen by 10 cents per trade after the new rules.         The time-series of our
estimate of market maker rent also show a decline of rent in early 1997 by
approximately 4 cents for entire sample. Several studies examine interdealer trading
(Reiss & Werner (1998)), preferencing, dealer profits and inventory (Hansch, Naik and
Viswanathan, (1998, 1999)) based on data from the London Stock Exchange.

2. Data
         The data contain monthly observations for all NASDAQ stocks during a period
from May 1995 to December 2000. NASDAQ provided monthly share trading volume
data of all dealers for each of stocks they are making markets in. We obtain trade and
quote data data from the NYSE’s TAQ database. To avoid problems with low trading
volume, we exclude all stocks on the SmallCap market. We also exclude, from stocks
on the NASDAQ National Market (NNM), stocks with average closing price below $2,
and with average daily dollar trading volume below $10,000. The number of stocks
each month in the sample ranges from 3,180 to 3,623.
         Table 1 contains descriptive statistics of the sample stocks. Panel A shows that
the distribution of daily dollar trading volume is heavily skewed to the right. Over
entire sample period, the mean daily dollar trading volume is $9,163,000, while the
median is only $475,500, which is just over 5% of the mean. Daily dollar trading
volume has increased by approximately 7-fold, from $3.29 million in 1995 to $23.4
million in 2000.
         The average trade size, however, has declined from $27,382 in 1995 to $15,358
in 2000.     The mean of trade size over entire sample is $22,709 and the median is
$15,185. A big increase in daily dollar trading volume and a decline in average trade
size suggests that there has been a huge increase in number of trades. Panel C shows
that it is indeed the case. Daily number of trades has gone up close to 10 times, from
71.8 in 1995 to 702.6 in 2000.     The increase in daily dollar trading volume is due to
more trades rather than bigger trades.         Schultz (2002) has also observed a similar
pattern in his sample of entire NASDAQ stocks over a period from May 1995 to
February 1998.

       Last two variables in Table 1 are measures of competition among market makers.
Panel G shows number of active market makers. Ellis et al (2002) observe that almost
one third of all market makers do not trade even once a week. Accordingly, we count
number of active market makers, defined as those with more than 0.1% share in total
trading volume for a stock in a given month. Overall, the number of active market
makers has increased from 11.6 in 1995 to 16.5 in 2000.              The mean and median
numbers of market makers are 13.2 and 11 over entire sample period. Previous studies
(See Stoll (1978) and Wahal (1997) for instance) used the number of market makers as
a measure of competition. In this paper, we use the Herfindhal-Hirschman index (HHI)
as the measure of competition. The HHI for stock i during month t is defined as


                            HHI i,t = Σ j = 1, N (Sj,t)2                             (2)


where Sj,t is the percentage of stock i’s trading volume handled by market maker j
during month t, and N denotes total number of market makers for stock i. For instance,



                                              5
if four market makers handle 25% each of stock i’s trading volume, then the HHI =
(25)2 + (25)2 + (25)2 + (25)2 = 2,500. It is easy to see that there are two components in
the HHI:


       HHI = 10,000* [ 1/N + N*(Variance of market makers’ market share) ]        (3)


According to equation (3), two factors affecting the HHI are the number of market
makers and how market shares are distributed among market makers, as measured by
the variance of market shares. If there are four market makers each handling 25% of
trading volume, then variance in eq. (3) is equal to zero, and HHI is equal to 2,500.
When distribution of market shares deviates from the equal market share case, variance
will become non-zero. As a result, the HHI will be higher than 2,500. The HHI is
widely used as a measure of competition or concentration at an industry level both by
researcher and regulatory body. For instance, the Federal Trade Commission uses a
guideline that a HHI below 1,000 indicates no concentration, HHI between 1,000-1,800
moderate concentration and HHI over 1,800 severe concentration. The guideline of the
Federal Trade Commission should be helpful in interpreting the HHI as applied to the
NASDAQ market making.
       Panel H shows the HHI. HHI has steadily decreased during sample period from
2,261 in 1995 to 1,748 in 2000. (See also Figure 1A.) The decline of HHI appears to
occur over a broad range of NNM stocks. Each month, we classify stocks into three $-
volume groups according to their dollar trading volume. Top 200 stocks in terms of $-
trading volume are classified as $-volume group 3, next 800 stocks as $-volume group 2
and the rest $-volume group 1. As Figure 1B shows, HHI has declined for all these
three $-volume groups. Note also that the HHI is skewed to the right: The mean of
HHI is 2,133 while the median is 1,734. Lastly, as Schultz (2002) points out, the
number of market makers relative to the HHI overstates the degree of competition: the
mean HHI of 2,133 roughly corresponds to having five market makers with equal share,
while the mean number of market makers is 13.2. Table 2 shows correlation among key
variables.

3. Concentration and Trading Costs
       Competition among market makers is the central feature of a dealer market such
as the NASDAQ. Indeed, the following statement of the NASDAQ fully recognizes the
importance of competition:


       “Key to NASDAQ's market structure are a core group of financial firms
       called market makers. … The aggressive competition for orders fostered
       among NASDAQ's market participants helps to ensure that investors
       receive the best prices for the securities they trade.”

In a competitive market, market makers would be able to wield only minimal market
power in setting the bid and ask prices. Even when they do exercise significant market
power and thus set wide spreads, it should not be persistent. Wide spreads will attract
new entries and foster more competition to bring the spreads down.          Therefore, a
measure of concentration would not affect trading costs in a competitive market. In
order to examine the effect of concentration on trading costs, we run the following
cross-sectional regression each month of the sample period,

Herfindahl index for the month, ln(TSizei) is the natural logarithm of the mean trade
size in dollar, ln(Ntradesi) the natural logarithm of the mean number of trades, σ2i
variance of stock prices, E(Pi) the mean closing price, and Ni the number of market
makers for stock i. We use the following estimate of the variance of stock prices:

       The effect of concentration on trading costs is not trivial.        The mean price,

of HHI from 2,000 to 1000 is associated with a decrease of percentage effective spread
by 0.257% (0.257*(2,000-1,000)/1,000 = 0.257%).          This corresponds to a decrease of
effective spread by ¢4.61 ($17.92*0.257% = ¢4.61), or 15% of the mean effective
spreads (¢4.61 ÷ ¢30.75 = 15%).

       In sum, we provide evidence that concentration does affect trading costs even
after controlling for usual explanatory variables. The effects are statistically significant.
Point estimate implies non-trivial significance in economic sense. The remaining issue
is to examine the economic significance of the effect of concentration.           If they are
indeed economically significant, an interesting issue is the longevity of the effect: will

among market makers? We examine these issues next.




4. Economic Significance and Persistence of Rent
      In previous section, we provide evidence that the degree of concentration affects
trading costs. Our interpretation of the result is that market makers exercise market
power in setting bid and ask prices, and they are able to extract rent. We examine in
this section the economic significance and persistence of rent.


A. Economic Significance of Rent
      We define our measure of rent as follows:


             Percentage Rent = (HHI/1,000)*Regression Coefficient                 (6)
             Rent = Average Closing Price * Percentage Rent.                      (7)


Percentage rent is the proportion of effective spread attributable to market power,
expressed in percentage of closing price. For instance, percentage rent of 0.1% for a
$18 stock implies that effective spread includes the rent of ¢1.8 ($18*0.1% = ¢1.8).
For stock i, we estimate the percentage rent each month by multiplying the HHIi with
cross-sectional regression coefficient on HHI for that month. We use the product of
percentage rent and average stock price as our estimate of rent (in cent) for stock i for
that month. Once we estimate the percentage rent and rent (in cent) for all sample
stocks over entire sample period, we calculate time-series mean of the percentage rent
and rent (in cent). (See Figures 2-5 for time-series mean.) Figure 2 reveals a break
point of the time series:   up until February 1997, the rents move around ¢12, and
thereafter rents have declined and fluctuated around ¢8.    The decline of rent shown in
Figure 2 is a result of the implementation of new order handling rules. (NASDAQ
implemented first two phases of new order handling rules on January 20 and February
10, 1997.)   Weston (2000) also observes the drop in economic rent following the

using the approach of Huang and Stoll (1997). He estimates combined order processing

      Figure 3 shows rents by dollar trading volume group. It appears that rent is about

B. Persistence of Rent
       Results in previous section suggest that market makers exercise market power in
setting bid and ask prices, and that the magnitude of rent is non-trivial. It will be also
important for both investors and regulators to know how quickly or slowly these rents
dissipate. If they dissipate quickly via intense competition, then the presence of market
power would be of less concern. However, if they are persistent, it is a different story.
In this section, we examine the persistence of rent.
       As our measure of persistence, we use half-life of percentage rent based on three-
month moving average of percentage rent.         Columns (4) and (5) show mean and
standard deviation of half-life.   For some stocks, their percentage rents have never
dropped below one-half of initial level. Mean and standard deviation in columns (4)
and (5) are only those stocks which have reached the level for half-life during sample
period.   Column (6) shows the percentage of those stocks out of entire sample stocks.
For entire NASDAQ sample (Panel A), average half-life is 20.3 months for 79.4% of
sample stocks.    Panel B shows half-life for dollar trading volume groups: 23 months
for high volume group, 20.6 months for medium volume group, and 20.2 months for
low volume group. Half-life would be closely related with how stable the competitive
environment for the market making activities are.      For stocks in high and low trading
volume groups, the competitive environment would be stable. The difference would be
that high volume group stocks would face more stiff competition and low percentage
rent, while low volume group stocks would face more concentration among a few
dealers and high percentage rent. Consequently, we expect to see a shorter half-life for
stocks in medium volume group than those for high and low volume groups, which is
consistent with result in Panel B. Note that half-life of Group 1 stocks is 20.2 months,
which appears to be shorter than that for Group 2 stocks. But the number is the average
of only 77.4% of Group 1 stocks, which have reached the level for half-life during
sample period.
       Panel C shows half-life of groups classified by year of initial public offering.
Since “old” stocks with IPOs prior to 1995 are likely to be at more mature stage in
terms of competitive environment, those stocks would have longer half-life than
“younger” stocks. Indeed, half-life of “old” stocks is 24.1 months, longer than average



                                          12
of 20.3 months for entire sample.      Half-lives of “younger” stocks with IPOs during
1995-1997 range from 12.6 - 17.8 months.
         In sum, our results suggest that dominant market makers are able to extract rent
in setting bid-ask spread. Our estimate of the rent is approximately 0.54% of stock
price.    Half-life of the percentage rent is approximately 20 months for entire sample,
while half-life of younger stocks tend to be shorter than those of more mature stocks.
Perhaps, it might take a while before a market maker can build up infrastructure to
attract order flows, as Schultz suggests. In the meantime, existing market makers would
be able to exercise market power and appear to earn abnormally large profits.


5. Conclusion
         Recent studies suggest that trading costs are increasing in market concentration
in market-making ((Ellis, Michaely and O’Hara (2002), Schultz (2002), Klock and
McCormick (1999)). This finding implies that dealers set wider bid-ask spreads than
they would in a competitive market, and dealers benefit from the wide spreads. Given
the central role of competition among market makers in a dealer market, good
understanding on the nature of market power, competition and trading costs
is of great importance to practitioners, researches and regulators.
         Using extensive dealer trading volume data, we examine how the dynamics of
market structure affect trading cost and aim to shed more light on the behavior of
market makers in NASDAQ. Our dataset covers around 3,500 NASDAQ stocks over a
period from May 1995 to December 2000. To the best of our knowledge, no studies
have yet examined time-series pattern in market power and competition in NASDAQ
based on such extensive data.
         We find that dominant market makers are able to extract rent in setting bid-ask
spread as much as 0.54% of stock price.        We feel that this is non-trivial magnitude.
The market power of dominant dealers appears to be persistent:           Half-life of the
percentage rent is approximately 20 months for entire sample. Our result is consistent
with Schultz (2002) who argues that dealers might need to develop necessary
infrastructure in order to capture a meaningful level of market share.      He identifies
access to order flow and informational advantage as two key elements of such


                                          13
infrastructure. While entry and exit in NASDAQ market making business appears to be
fluid with very low cost, it might take a while before a new entrant poses a significant
competition to existing dominant market makers.

stock i is the time until the observatio of % rent for stock i halves. Column (4) reports
cross-sectional average of half-life for stock i, Column (5) reports standard deviation.
Column (6) reports the percentage of stocks which have reached the level of % rent for
