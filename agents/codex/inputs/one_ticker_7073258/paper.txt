One Ticker Deep: The Trend Strategy That Passed Every Test
              and Still Shouldn't Be Believed
                                      Author: Prashant Malan
                                          Draft: July 2026
Companion paper to "Do Simple Cross-Sectional Trend Strategies Survive Honest Validation? Evidence
                           from a Small US Large-Cap Universe, 2020–2024."



Abstract
In a companion study I showed that two canonical cross-sectional long-short strategies — moving-
average crossover and 12-1 momentum — fail walk-forward validation on US large-caps over 2020–
2024, and that a single train/test split overstates their performance roughly threefold. This paper
studies the strategy that passed. A directional, long-only time-series trend rule — hold the strongest
names trading above their own moving average, retreat to cash otherwise — survives the identical
walk-forward protocol with an out-of-sample Sharpe of 1.36 on the original 26-name universe
(CAGR 34.1% against 20.8% for the S&P 500), and 1.53 on a broadened 99-name universe selected
on start-of-sample information. Every cell of its 36-point parameter grid beats buy-and-hold in-
sample. By the standards of the companion paper, this strategy is validated. I then attribute the
result, in three layers. Volatility terciles reveal that the performance is monotone in volatility and
lives entirely in the highest-volatility third of the universe; in the lower two terciles the strategy
underperforms buy-and-hold. Excluding a single stock — NVIDIA — from either universe collapses
the walk-forward Sharpe below the S&P 500's (1.13 and 1.05 versus 1.30) and shrinks the CAGR
edge from over thirteen percentage points to about two. Repairing the survivorship bias of the
universe does not repair the concentration: the dependence on one historic outlier is a property of
the strategy, not of the stock list. As a cross-check, the same broadened universe fails to rescue the
long-short strategies (walk-forward Sharpes of −0.31 and 0.18), rejecting the breadth hypothesis
advanced in the companion paper. What survives every test is trend-following's drawdown
reduction — maximum drawdowns of −13% to −19% against −24% to −34% for buy-and-hold — at
roughly market-like returns. The methodological conclusion extends the companion paper's: a clean
validation protocol can still bless a lucky strategy. Validate, then attribute, before believing any
backtest.

Keywords: trend following, time-series momentum, backtest overfitting, attribution, survivorship
bias, concentration risk

1. Introduction
The companion paper to this one ends with a defensible negative: neither a moving-average
crossover nor classic cross-sectional momentum, implemented dollar-neutral on liquid US large-
caps, survives walk-forward validation over 2020–2024. The natural question a practitioner asks
next is whether the validation pipeline is simply too harsh — whether anything can pass it. This
paper answers that question, and the answer turns out to be more instructive than either a plain yes
or a plain no.

The candidate is the simplest directional strategy in the practitioner canon: time-series trend
following (Moskowitz, Ooi and Pedersen, 2012; Faber, 2007). Each stock is compared to its own
trailing moving average rather than to its peers. Names trading above their average are candidates to
hold; names below it are sold. The portfolio holds the few strongest candidates and retreats to cash
when trends die, so the book de-risks mechanically in broad downturns. Unlike the dollar-neutral
constructions of the companion paper, this strategy deliberately carries market beta; the fair
benchmark is therefore buy-and-hold itself, and the bar is to beat it.

By every standard applied in the companion paper, the strategy clears the bar. Its in-sample
parameter surface is not a lucky spike but a uniform plateau: all 36 combinations of trend window
and portfolio breadth beat the S&P 500's Sharpe ratio, with a median of 1.20 against 0.76. It survives
the walk-forward protocol that dismantled the long-short strategies, posting an out-of-sample
Sharpe of 1.36 with a fourteen-percentage-point CAGR advantage over the index. And when I repair
the most serious data defect of the companion study — a universe hand-picked in 2026, after the
sample — by re-selecting roughly one hundred names on January 2020 index membership, the walk-
forward result improves, to 1.53.

A researcher who stopped here would deploy this strategy. The contribution of this paper is to show,
in three attribution layers, why that would be a mistake — and what a more careful reading of the
same evidence actually supports.

First, sorting the universe into volatility terciles shows the performance is monotone in volatility
and concentrated entirely in the top tercile — the NVDA, META, AMZN cohort. In the bottom two
terciles, covering two-thirds of the universe, the strategy underperforms buy-and-hold. The pattern
replicates exactly on the survivorship-corrected universe. Whatever this strategy harvests, it is not a
universal trend effect; it is a property of high-volatility growth names in this particular regime.

Second, removing a single stock — NVIDIA, which rose roughly tenfold over the sample — collapses
the strategy's walk-forward Sharpe from 1.36 to 1.13 on the original universe and from 1.53 to 1.05
on the broadened one, in both cases below the S&P 500's 1.30 over the same span. The CAGR
advantage shrinks from +13.3 and +20.5 percentage points to +2.2 and +2.4. One ticker out of
twenty-six, or out of ninety-nine, accounts for essentially the entire risk-adjusted edge. Strikingly, the
ex-NVDA CAGR is almost identical across the two universes (23.0% versus 23.2%): remove the
outlier and the strategy converges to market-like returns regardless of the stock list.
Third, the broadened universe serves as a controlled experiment on the companion paper's leading
conjecture — that the long-short strategies failed for lack of cross-sectional breadth. The conjecture
is rejected: quadrupling the universe leaves momentum's walk-forward Sharpe essentially
unchanged (0.18 versus 0.22) and pushes the MA long-short negative (−0.31 versus 0.35). Breadth
was never the binding constraint; the signals were.

The residual finding, after all three layers, is modest and I believe genuine: the trend rule's cash-
retreat mechanism reliably truncates drawdowns (−13% to −19% across every specification, against
−24% to −34% for buy-and-hold) at roughly market-like returns. That is a defensible property with
independent support in the literature (Faber, 2007; Moskowitz, Ooi and Pedersen, 2012). It is also a
much smaller claim than the headline backtest appears to support.

The paper's method is its message. The companion study showed that the choice of validation
protocol can fabricate an edge; this study shows that even a sound protocol can certify one that
attribution immediately dissolves. Walk-forward validation asks whether a strategy would have
worked. Attribution asks why — and a "why" of "one historic stock happened to be in the list" is not
an edge, however clean the protocol that reported it.

Section 2 describes the data and the two universes. Section 3 specifies the strategy and recapitulates
the shared evaluation machinery. Section 4 presents results in the order the experiments were run
— baseline, parameter surface, validation, then the three attribution layers — and closes with a
master comparison of every scenario generated. Section 5 discusses interpretation, Section 6
limitations, and Section 7 concludes. Appendix A contains the strategy and attribution source code;
Appendix B gives replication instructions.



2. Data and universes

2.1 Source

All data are split- and dividend-adjusted daily bars from the Alpaca Market Data API, 2 January 2020
to 30 December 2024 (1,257 trading days), with fields open, high, low, close, volume, trade count,
and daily VWAP. Data-integrity checks (adjustment verification at known splits; VWAP within [low,
high] on 100% of rows) are described in the companion paper. SPY proxies buy-and-hold and is
never tradable.

2.2 The two universes: a controlled comparison

The companion paper's universe — 26 liquid large-caps across five sectors — was hand-picked in
2026, i.e. after the sample. That is a textbook survivorship defect: the list contains the names known,
with hindsight, to have remained large and liquid, and conspicuously includes NVIDIA. For a dollar-
neutral book the defect is partially self-hedging (both the longs and the shorts come from the same
flattered list). For a long-only strategy that concentrates in recent winners it is potentially fatal,
which motivates the second universe.
The 2020 universe. Ninety-nine names reconstructing S&P 100 membership as of January 2020 —
selection using nothing known after the sample begins. It therefore includes the subsequent losers a
2026 list quietly drops: Boeing, General Electric, Walgreens, Intel, Disney, PayPal, Citigroup, 3M and
others. All 99 names have full 1,257-day coverage on Alpaca.

Two residual biases are documented rather than hidden. Members delisted mid-sample through
corporate actions (Allergan, acquired May 2020; Raytheon/United Technologies, merged April 2020)
could not be included because the data vendor does not serve delisted tickers; the list therefore still
slightly overweights firms that survived 2020–2024 as independent companies (Shumway, 1997,
documents the general form of this bias). And membership is reconstructed from public sources
rather than a licensed point-in-time constituents file, so small errors are possible. Neither caveat
affects the paper's central comparisons, which hold the universe fixed while varying the strategy, or
vice versa.



3. Strategy and methodology

3.1 The trend rule
For stock i on day t, with an n-day simple moving average:

     score_it = P_it / SMA_n(P_i)_t − 1

A positive score means the stock trades above its own n-day average — the time-series definition of
an uptrend, as distinct from the cross-sectional ranking of the companion paper, in which the same
arithmetic would be compared across stocks. Here the zero line carries meaning: it is the buy/sell
boundary.

Each rebalance (every 5 trading days), among stocks with positive scores, the n_hold strongest are
held at fixed weight 1/ n_hold each. If fewer than n_hold names qualify, the shortfall remains in
cash; if none qualify, the book is entirely in cash. Sizing is deliberately fixed rather than
renormalised, so gross exposure floats between 0 and 100% with the breadth of the uptrend — this
is the mechanism that de-risks the book in broad selloffs without any dedicated crash logic.
Positions are held constant between rebalances. The default configuration is a 100-day window
holding 5 names; both parameters are swept in Section 4.2.

3.2 Shared machinery

The backtest engine, cost model and validation protocols are identical to the companion paper, to
which I refer for detail: no look-ahead (weights lag their signal by one day), costs of 5 bps per unit of
gross turnover, close-to-close accounting, Sharpe with the risk-free rate at zero (noted as a
convention; for a long-only book this flatters slightly more than for a dollar-neutral one). The two
out-of-sample protocols are likewise unchanged: a 60/40 chronological train/test split, and a walk-
forward that re-selects parameters every 126 trading days on a trailing 378-day window,
concatenating the out-of-sample blocks. The parameter grid for selection is window ∈ {20, 40, 60,
80, 100, 125, 150, 200, 250} × n_hold ∈ {3, 5, 8, 10}, 36 combinations.

3.3 Attribution protocols

Three attribution layers, fixed before inspecting their results:

Volatility terciles. Rank the universe by full-sample annualised volatility, split into thirds, and run the
strategy independently within each tercile. Using full-sample volatility involves mild look-ahead
(rankings are, however, highly persistent); the diagnostic purpose is to locate the performance, not
to propose a tradable sort.

Single-name exclusion. Re-run the entire walk-forward — selection and all — on the universe minus
its largest single contributor, NVIDIA. If a strategy's edge is a portfolio property, removing one of 26
(or one of 99) names should dent it modestly; if the edge collapses, the "portfolio strategy" was a
single-stock bet wearing a systematic costume.

Universe replacement. Re-run the walk-forward on the 2020 universe, with and without NVIDIA, and
— as a cross-check on the companion paper's breadth conjecture — re-run the two long-short
strategies on the broadened universe as well.

Two structural observations. First, the horizon lesson of the companion paper recurs for the fifth
time across these studies: the 20-day column is the worst on the surface, and the comparison of the
two extreme cells isolates the mechanism. Both run identical volatility (25.3%), but the 20-day cell
earns barely half the return with 2.4 times the turnover — it whipsaws, selling after dips and re-
buying after rebounds, so it suffers a worse maximum drawdown than buy-and-hold (−35.7% versus
−33.8%) despite being a "risk-managed" strategy. Days-scale signals are noise; the viable trend
horizon is months.

Second, and more importantly: a 36-for-36 sweep on a universe selected after the sample should be
read as a red flag, not a robustness certificate. When every variant of a long-only "hold the strongest"
rule beats the market on a list containing the era's biggest winners, the natural hypothesis is that the
list, not the rule, is doing the work. Sections 4.4–4.6 test that hypothesis directly.

The pattern is identical on both universes and admits no ambiguity: performance rises
monotonically with volatility, and the effect lives entirely in the top tercile. In the low and medium
terciles — two-thirds of either universe — trend-following underperforms buy-and-hold. "Trend-
following worked in 2020–2024" is the wrong description of these tables. "Trend-following high-
volatility growth stocks worked in 2020–2024" is the right one, and it is a materially weaker claim: it
identifies the return engine as a specific, regime-dependent cohort, not a universal effect. The broad-
universe replication adds one sobering detail: the clean universe's high-vol tercile suffered a −38.6%
maximum drawdown — worse than buy-and-hold — where the 2026-picked list showed only
−23.2%. The survivorship-tainted list had erased not just losers but the true downside of the
winners' cohort.

The volatility sort also suggests why every fold of the walk-forward selects maximal concentration:
the fewer names the book holds, the higher the share of the era's few explosive winners. Which
raises the obvious question of how much of the edge is attributable to the single most explosive one.

4.6 Attribution III: the universe fix does not fix the strategy — and rejects the breadth
conjecture
The 99-name results above double as the survivorship experiment. Note the direction of the effect:
on the cleaner universe, the with-NVDA result got better (1.53 versus 1.36; CAGR 41.3%, drawdown
−13.4%, no negative folds), because 73 additional 2020-era names gave the selector more high-vol
winners to rotate through. The survivorship correction, by itself, would have increased confidence in
the strategy. Only the exclusion test reveals that the improved number rests on the same single-name
foundation (1.53 → 1.05). Concentration risk is a property of the strategy — a top-3 book will find
NVIDIA in any universe that contains it — and no amount of universe hygiene removes it.

5. Discussion

5.1 Why validation certified a lucky strategy

The walk-forward protocol did nothing wrong. At every fold it used only trailing information; its
selections were reasonable; its out-of-sample accounting was clean. It certified the strategy because,
within the data it was shown, the strategy genuinely would have worked. The failure is one of scope:
the protocol answers "would this rule, applied to this universe, have beaten the index?" — a question
whose answer can be dominated by a single constituent. NVIDIA's ascent was real, and a
concentrated trend rule genuinely would have ridden it. What the protocol cannot say is whether "a
stock like NVIDIA exists in the universe and the strategy concentrates into it" is a repeatable
mechanism or a one-draw event. That is an attribution question, and it required exactly three cheap
experiments to answer: sort by the suspected driver (volatility), delete the suspected driver (NVDA),
and change the sampling frame (the 2020 universe). Each experiment reuses the existing pipeline;
together they took minutes of compute. There is no methodological excuse for omitting them, and
the literature's warnings about multiple testing (Harvey, Liu and Zhu, 2016; Bailey and Ló pez de
Prado, 2014) apply with equal force to this subtler, single-asset form of luck.

5.2 What the strategy actually is

The three layers agree on a description. The rule is a momentum-concentration device: it channels
the portfolio into whatever the universe's highest-volatility uptrend is, and 2020–2024 supplied a
historic one. Its Sharpe advantage is an artefact of that supply. Its drawdown advantage, by contrast,
is mechanical — the cash-retreat triggers whenever broad trends break, which is a property of the
rule, not of any particular stock — and accordingly it survives every attribution layer: −18.6%,
−13.4%, −19.2%, −16.7% across specifications, against −24% to −34% for buy-and-hold. This aligns
with the established reading of time-series momentum as primarily a tail-risk modifier (Moskowitz,
Ooi and Pedersen, 2012; Faber, 2007): the honest residual claim is "market-like returns with
materially smaller drawdowns," not "34% a year."
It is worth stating what would have to be true for the bigger claim to stand. The strategy would need
high-volatility trend winners to exist, be identifiable ex ante, and recur across regimes — in effect, a
stationary supply of NVIDIAs. The sample contains one such episode, which is exactly enough to
produce these backtests and exactly too few to support the claim.

5.3 The breadth conjecture, and the value of negative controls
The companion paper closed by conjecturing that its long-short failures reflected universe breadth.
Table 6 rejects this, and the rejection has independent value: it was produced by a negative control
— re-running unchanged strategies on the changed universe. Had the broadened universe rescued
momentum, the survivorship fix would have been confounded with the breadth effect; because
momentum stayed dead while trend-following improved, the two effects separate cleanly. Designing
universe changes so that they test more than one hypothesis at a time is cheap, and this paper
benefited from it twice.



6. Limitations
The attribution toolkit here is deliberately elementary — sorts, exclusions, and reruns — and stops
short of formal factor attribution; regressing the strategy's returns on market, size, momentum and
volatility factors would quantify what the sorts only locate. The volatility terciles use full-sample
volatility (mild look-ahead, diagnostic only). The 2020 universe cannot include mid-sample
delistings, leaving residual survivorship of the Shumway (1997) type. Costs remain 5 bps on
turnover with no borrow, impact, or capacity modelling — generous to all strategies, though least
distorting for the low-turnover trend rule (average daily turnover 0.09). The walk-forward has five
folds; fold-level dispersion (−0.69 to +3.26) implies wide error bands on every stitched number,
including the benchmark comparisons. All results concern one five-year window containing exactly
one regime cycle. And the exclusion test removes only the single largest contributor; a top-k
exclusion curve (k = 1, 2, 3, …) would trace the concentration profile more completely and is left for
the replication package.

None of these limitations rescues the headline claim; each either weakens it further or leaves it
unchanged. The drawdown-reduction finding is the one result robust to every variation tested.



7. Conclusion
A directional trend-following rule passed, with margin, the validation gauntlet that eliminated two
canonical long-short strategies in the companion paper: every parameter cell beat buy-and-hold in-
sample, and walk-forward out-of-sample Sharpes reached 1.36 on the original universe and 1.53 on
a survivorship-corrected one. Three attribution layers then reduced the claim to its true size: the
performance is confined to the highest-volatility tercile, collapses below the benchmark when one
stock is removed from either universe, and improves with universe hygiene only because a cleaner
list supplies the same concentration mechanism with more fuel. The durable residual is a modest,
well-precedented property — market-like returns with maximum drawdowns roughly a third
smaller than buy-and-hold — plus a rejected conjecture: universe breadth does not resurrect the
long-short signals.

The pair of papers now states a two-part discipline. The first showed that a favourable protocol can
fabricate out-of-sample evidence: one split reported triple the walk-forward's Sharpe. This one
shows that a sound protocol can still certify a lucky composition: the walk-forward's approval was
real, and one ticker deep. Validation asks whether a strategy would have worked; attribution asks
why; and a backtest deserves belief only after both questions have answers that survive each other.

A.1 The trend-following strategy ( research/strategies/trend_follow.py )

 """Directional (long-only) trend following — time-series momentum.

 Each stock is judged against its OWN trend: score = price / SMA(window) - 1.
 At each rebalance we hold the `n_hold` strongest names among those in an
 uptrend (score > 0), 1/n_hold weight each. If fewer than n_hold names are
 trending up, the remainder stays in cash — so the book de-risks automatically
 in broad downturns."""

 import numpy as np
 import pandas as pd



 def trend_score(prices: pd.DataFrame, window: int = 100) -> pd.DataFrame:
     return prices / prices.rolling(window).mean() - 1.0



 def weights(prices, window=100, n_hold=5, rebalance_days=5):
     score = trend_score(prices, window)

      target = pd.DataFrame(np.nan, index=prices.index, columns=prices.columns)
      rebal_dates = prices.index[window::rebalance_days]
      for dt in rebal_dates:
          row = score.loc[dt].dropna()
          up = row[row > 0].sort_values(ascending=False)
          w = pd.Series(0.0, index=prices.columns)
          if len(up) > 0:
              picks = up.index[:n_hold]
              w[picks] = 1.0 / n_hold      # fixed sizing; shortfall stays in cash
          target.loc[dt] = w
      return target.ffill().fillna(0.0)    # hold between rebalances

 def bucket_universe(prices):
     """Split the universe into volatility terciles (full-sample annualised
     vol; mild look-ahead, acceptable for a diagnostic sort)."""
     vol = (prices.pct_change().std() * np.sqrt(252)).sort_values()
     n = len(vol)
     return {
         "low":    list(vol.index[: n // 3]),
         "medium": list(vol.index[n // 3: 2 * n // 3]),
         "high":   list(vol.index[2 * n // 3:]),
     }
