Abstract

Systematic trading research overwhelmingly concentrates on
signal generation, the rules that determine direction, while
treating the moment of execution as a fixed consequence of the
signal rather than a decision variable in its own right. This
paper isolates entry timing as a distinct layer and tests one
specific, easily implementable variant: delaying entry until price
has moved a threshold percentage against the signal’s direction
(a pullback for long entries, a rally for short entries), using each
trade’s recorded Maximum Adverse Excursion (MAE, Sweeney,
1996) to determine, ex post, whether such a delayed limit order
would have filled. Applied to a proprietary systematic trend-
continuation signal traded on NSE Nifty index futures over a
fifteen-year sample (2010 to 2026, N = 3,908 trades), a modest
pullback-confirmation requirement (approximately 0.25% of the
underlying) improves the Sharpe ratio from 1.38 to 3.33 and
the Sortino ratio from 2.59 to 10.11, net of realistic transaction

costs, while retaining 63% of the original trade population. The
improvement is robust across a threshold band rather than
concentrated at a single point, and Monte Carlo resampling
confirms the result is not an artefact of favorable trade
ordering. We discuss the likely mechanism (adverse-selection
filtering of low-conviction signals), the opportunity cost
(profitable trades foregone when the required pullback never
materializes), and the boundary conditions under which this
technique should be expected to generalize.

1. Introduction

The academic and practitioner literature on systematic trading
is asymmetric in an easily overlooked way. A large body of work
addresses signal construction (which conditions should trigger
a trade) and a separate, largely disconnected body of work
addresses optimal execution of a decision already made,
primarily in the market microstructure literature on order
placement and slicing (Almgren & Chriss, 2000; Guo, de
Larrard, & Ruan, 2017). Comparatively little attention sits
between these two layers: given that a signal has fired, is the
instant of firing actually the optimal moment to act on it, or is
there a cheap, testable, rule-based way to wait for confirmation
without forfeiting the edge the signal was designed to capture?

This question is distinct from execution-cost minimization in the
microstructure sense. It is not about how to fill a given order at
the lowest impact cost; it is about whether the order should be
placed at all at the moment the signal fires, versus at a specific,



                                 2
worse price that the signal must first reach. This is closer in
spirit to discretionary trading heuristics (“don’t chase, let it
come to you”) than to formal execution theory, and to our
knowledge it has not been subjected to a rigorous, cost-
adjusted, out-of-sample-style statistical test using a real multi-
year trade population and a well-established excursion metric.

We use Maximum Adverse Excursion (MAE), introduced by
Sweeney (1996, 1997) as a tool for empirically calibrating stop-
loss placement, for a different purpose: to determine, for a
completed historical trade, whether a hypothetical resting limit
order at a specified adverse offset from the original signal price
would have been filled before the trade’s actual exit. This
allows a clean, non-parametric simulation of a delayed-entry
rule without requiring tick-level order book reconstruction.

1.1 Contribution

This paper makes three contributions. First, it formalizes a
simple, MAE-based method for testing delayed, confirmation-
based entry rules on any existing trade log, requiring only entry
price, exit price, and per-trade MAE, data that most trade-
management platforms already record. Second, it provides a
full empirical test on a real, multi-year systematic strategy,
including realistic transaction costs and Monte Carlo
robustness checks, rather than a stylized illustration. Third, it
documents that the effect, where present, is not knife-edge; the
improvement holds across a range of thresholds, which is
evidence against the result being a curve-fitting artefact.

1.2 A Note on Disclosure

The underlying trading signal to which this entry-timing overlay
is applied is proprietary and its specific rules are withheld,
consistent with standard practice for practitioner-oriented
empirical work of this kind. What is fully disclosed, and what
this paper’s contribution rests on, is the entry-timing

strategy.


2. Related Work

Sweeney (1996, 1997) introduced Maximum Adverse Excursion
as a diagnostic for calibrating stop-loss levels: by plotting MAE
against final trade outcome across a large trade sample, a
trader can identify the adverse-excursion level beyond which
trades rarely recover, informing where a protective stop should
sit. Subsequent practitioner literature has applied MAE (and its
counterpart, Maximum Favorable Excursion, MFE) primarily to
exit-side decisions, stop placement and profit-target calibration,
with comparatively little published work using MAE to inform
entry-side decisions.

The market microstructure literature offers a related but
distinct line of work on optimal order placement in a limit order
book (Guo, de Larrard, & Ruan, 2017; Almgren & Chriss, 2000),
which typically takes the decision to trade as given and
optimizes the mechanics of execution, order size, timing within
a fixed window, and limit versus market order mix, to minimize
cost and market impact. This literature generally does not
address whether the initiating signal itself should be delayed,
since the models typically begin from a mandate to complete a
given trade within a horizon (e.g., liquidating a position), rather
than from a discretionary systematic signal that could simply be
skipped if not confirmed.




                                 4
Discretionary trading heuristics around waiting for a pullback
or retracement before entering a trend are widely discussed in
practitioner and educational trading literature (e.g.,
retracement-based entry frameworks common in technical
analysis), but this literature is generally not statistically
rigorous: claims are illustrated with selected chart examples
rather than tested across a full trade population with cost
adjustment and robustness analysis. This paper aims to bring
the same empirical standard applied to signal research to this
specific, execution-adjacent question.


3. Data and Methodology

3.1 Data

The sample consists of 3,908 closed round-trip positions
generated by a proprietary systematic trend-continuation signal
traded on NSE Nifty 50 index futures, hourly bar resolution,
from 13 April 2010 to 30 January 2026 (approximately 15.8
years). Each record includes the signal direction (long or short),
entry price and timestamp, exit price and timestamp, and the
trade’s Maximum Adverse Excursion, defined as the worst price
the position touched (in the position’s unrealized loss direction)
at any point between entry and exit. All returns are computed
as a percentage of entry price rather than in index points,
ensuring comparability across the roughly fivefold range the
underlying index traversed over the sample period.

3.2 Base Strategy

The base signal’s specific entry and exit rules are proprietary
and are not disclosed in this paper (Section 1.2). For the
purpose of this study it should be treated as a black box that
produces a sequence of directional trade decisions, each with a
known entry price, exit price, and intra-trade MAE; the overlay
methodology tested here does not depend on, and is not specific




                                5
to, any detail of how that signal is generated. The base signal’s
own unfiltered, cost-adjusted performance (Sharpe ratio 1.38,
annualized) is reported in Section 4.1 purely as the baseline
against which the entry-timing overlay is evaluated.

3.3 Pullback-Confirmation Overlay

For a chosen threshold θ (expressed as a percentage of the
entry price), a trade is redefined as follows:

 • If the trade’s MAE was at least as deep as θ (i.e., price
   moved against the signal by θ or more at some point before
   the original exit), the trade is retained. Its entry price is
   reset to the level θ away from the original signal price in the
   adverse direction, and its return is recalculated from that
   revised entry to the original, unchanged exit price.
 • If the trade’s MAE never reached θ, the trade is excluded
   from the filtered sample entirely, on the interpretation that a
   resting limit order placed at the θ offset would never have
   been filled; price moved directly in the signal’s favor without
   offering the required confirmation.

This is directly and realistically implementable as a live trading
rule: upon signal generation, submit a limit order at the entry
price adjusted by θ against the signal direction, with a time-in-
force matching the strategy’s typical holding horizon, and
cancel if unfilled. The method assumes that a resting limit order
at the θ level would fill without additional slippage once price
trades through it, and that price moves continuously through
the θ level en route to the recorded MAE, both standard,
defensible assumptions at hourly bar resolution, though not
certainties.




                                6
3.4 Transaction Costs

All performance figures reported net of cost apply a round-trip
transaction cost of 6.02 basis points per trade, comprising
India-specific statutory charges (Securities Transaction Tax,
exchange transaction charges, stamp duty, applicable at the
time of writing) and an assumed slippage allowance of two
index points, consistent with typical execution quality for
hourly-bar-frequency systematic trading on this instrument.
This cost is subtracted from every trade in both the baseline
and filtered samples, ensuring the comparison in Section 4 is
not an artefact of ignoring execution frictions.

3.5 Performance Metrics and Robustness Testing

Standard performance metrics are reported: compound annual
growth rate (CAGR), annualized Sharpe ratio (monthly returns,
risk-free rate = 0), annualized Sortino ratio (downside deviation
only), Calmar ratio (CAGR divided by maximum drawdown),
maximum drawdown, win rate, and profit factor. Robustness is
assessed via Monte Carlo simulation: the retained trade
sequence is randomly reordered 10,000 times (trade-order
permutation), holding the set of trade returns fixed, to
characterize the distribution of maximum drawdown under
alternative, equally likely historical orderings, following
standard practice in systematic strategy evaluation.


4. Results

4.1 Baseline Performance

Net of transaction cost, the unfiltered base signal produced a
CAGR of 12.3%, an annualized Sharpe ratio of 1.38, a Sortino
ratio of 2.59, a Calmar ratio of 1.06, and a maximum drawdown
of 11.6% over the sample period, with a win rate of 52.4% and a
profit factor of 1.27.

Performance improves monotonically from the unfiltered
baseline up to θ ≈ 0.25%, where Sharpe peaks at 3.33 (a 141%
relative improvement over baseline) and Sortino reaches 10.11
(baseline 2.59), before gradually declining as further tightening
of the threshold reduces the retained sample to a point where
fewer, higher-conviction trades no longer compensate, in
aggregate compounding terms, for the reduction in trading
frequency.

4.3 Robustness

The improvement is not concentrated at a single, narrow
threshold. Sharpe exceeds 3.2 across the entire 0.15% to 0.35%
band (Table 1 and interpolated values), a range wide enough
that the result is unlikely to reflect fitting to a single, favorable




                                    8
data point. Trade-order permutation (10,000 resamples) of the
θ = 0.25% configuration places the realized historical maximum
drawdown (10.8%) at the 87th percentile of the simulated
distribution (i.e., worse than 87% of random reorderings of the
same trades), indicating the historical result was not an
unusually fortunate sequencing of an otherwise weaker
underlying trade population.

4.4 Directional Decomposition

Applying the identical methodology separately to long and short
trades shows the confirmation effect holds in both directions,
though of different magnitude: long trades improve from a
baseline Sharpe of 1.41 to a peak of 2.09 (θ ≈ 0.10 to 0.15%),
while short trades improve from a materially weaker baseline
(Sharpe 0.44) to a peak of 2.58 (θ ≈ 0.25 to 0.35%), the largest
relative improvement of either leg. This is consistent with the
confirmation filter disproportionately screening out lower-
conviction entries, an effect that is naturally more visible in the
segment (short trades, in this sample) that started with more
low-conviction entries to screen out.


5. Discussion

5.1 Candidate Mechanism

We propose three, non-mutually-exclusive explanations for the
observed improvement. First, a mechanical entry-price effect:
by construction, every retained trade is entered at a price that
has already moved in the trade’s eventual favor relative to the
original signal price, which alone improves realized average
return per trade, holding the signal’s directional accuracy
constant. Second, an adverse-selection or confirmation effect: a
signal that survives an initial adverse move before proceeding
in the predicted direction may reflect stronger underlying
conviction (e.g., more committed order flow, less likely to be




                                 9
transient noise) than a signal that moves directly and
immediately in the predicted direction, which could equally
reflect a short-lived, low-conviction move. Third, a selection
effect on signal quality: if the base signal’s true edge is
heterogeneous across trades, and lower-edge trades are more
likely to move immediately without a pullback (because they
lack the conviction to sustain an initial retracement test), the
confirmation filter acts as an implicit, non-parametric quality
filter, which is consistent with the disproportionate
improvement observed on this study’s weaker (short) leg.

Distinguishing empirically between these three mechanisms is
beyond the scope of this paper and is a natural direction for
future work, for instance by testing whether the effect persists
when the entry price is held fixed at the original signal price
(isolating the selection effect from the mechanical entry-price
effect).

5.2 The Cost of Confirmation: Foregone Trades

At the optimal threshold identified in this sample (θ = 0.25%),
37% of signals are never converted into trades because the
required pullback never materialized before the original exit.
These are, by construction, trades that would have been
profitable at immediate entry; the technique deliberately
forgoes them in exchange for improved average quality and a
better risk-adjusted profile on the trades it does take. Whether
this trade-off is favorable is an empirical question specific to
each strategy’s trade-return distribution and cannot be
assumed to generalize; a strategy whose edge derives primarily
from a small number of large, immediate moves (rather than
from the interaction between conviction and pullback frequency
documented here) could plausibly be harmed by this technique
rather than helped.

5.3 Practical Implementation and Limitations

 • This is a backtested simulation using historical MAE data,
   not a live-execution study; actual limit-order fill rates and
   any additional slippage on the confirmation fill itself are not
   modeled and should be validated before capital deployment.
 • The method assumes intrabar price continuity through the
   confirmation level, a reasonable approximation at hourly
   resolution that may not hold at lower (e.g., daily) bar
   frequency, where a gap could jump past the confirmation
   level without a valid fill opportunity, or at much higher (tick)
   frequency, where the underlying MAE recording granularity
   would need to match.
 • The specific optimal threshold (θ ≈ 0.25% in this sample) is
   a property of this particular signal’s trade-return
   distribution, its typical holding period, and the volatility
   regime of the instrument tested; it should not be treated as
   a universal constant, and any application to a different
   strategy or instrument requires re-estimating the threshold
   sweep documented in Section 4.2 on that strategy’s own
   data.
 • As with any single-sample historical backtest, results are
   subject to the general risks of regime change, structural
   market shifts, and, if the technique becomes widely adopted,
   potential erosion of the effect through crowding.


6. Conclusion

This paper isolates entry timing as a distinct, testable layer
separate from both signal generation and microstructure-level
execution optimization, and proposes a simple, MAE-based
methodology for evaluating a specific entry-timing rule:
delaying entry until a signal has been confirmed by a threshold
adverse price move. Applied to a real systematic trading signal
over a fifteen-year sample, the technique produced a large,
cost-adjusted improvement in risk-adjusted performance



                                11
(Sharpe 1.38 to 3.33; Sortino 2.59 to 10.11) that is robust
across a range of thresholds and survives Monte Carlo
resequencing analysis, at the acknowledged cost of foregoing a
meaningful share of trades that would have been profitable
without the confirmation requirement.

The broader implication is that entry timing deserves treatment
as its own research question in systematic trading, distinct from
and complementary to signal design, particularly given how
inexpensively it can be tested on data that most systematic
trading operations already collect. We encourage other
practitioners to apply the methodology in Section 3.3 to their
own trade logs; the technique requires no new data
infrastructure beyond what MAE tracking already provides,
and, as this paper demonstrates, the result can be large enough
to warrant the investigation.
