Abstract
This paper investigates the empirical profitability of a cross-border pairs trading
strategy utilizing leveraged Exchange-Traded Funds (ETFs) listed in the US and UK
that track identical underlying indices and specifically focuses on the pair of TQQQ
and LQQ3. Despite the theoretical Law of One Price, we identify persistent
short-term price divergences driven by jurisdictional liquidity shifts and time-zone
imbalances. Utilizing a Z-score-based mean-reversion model, we demonstrate that
these discrepancies offer significant market-neutral alpha, performing consistently in
both bullish and bearish regimes. However, we also conduct an analysis of the
impacts of transaction costs and bid-ask spreads, which we find can greatly erode
profits for retail investors who do not have access to an institutional grade set up with
advantage of scale.



1. Introduction
Given the majority of hedge fund activity and academic research focuses on
intra-market equity pairs within the US, what are the returns from following a
cross-border strategy that pairs identical indices across different jurisdictions? Is
there a persistent pricing dislocation between the US and the UK for the same
underlying asset, and if so, can it be explained by the structural differences between
these two major markets? In this paper, we attempt to address these questions by
examining the returns and risk of a specific type of statistical arbitrage known as
pairs trading, utilizing a dual-listed sample of leveraged ETFs tracking the
Nasdaq-100 index: TQQQ (listed in the US) and LQQ3 (listed in the UK).
During the first quarter of 2026, global equity markets experienced significant
volatility and shifting liquidity regimes. Traditional long-only strategies often faced
considerable drawdowns during this period. According to broader market
benchmarks, the early months of 2026 were characterized by rapid price swings that
challenged conventional portfolio management. Specific Equity Market Neutral
(EMN) strategies, however, are designed to remain indifferent to such directional
volatility. This led to a greater focus on market-neutral strategies that could provide
steady returns. Pairs trading, in particular, has historically been used to navigate
these conditions by isolating relative value rather than betting on market direction.

Pairs trading is typically classified as an EMN investment strategy based on
short-term price reversal. Characterized in industry as a form of statistical arbitrage,
the strategy involves finding two securities that follow a similar trading pattern. When
the price relationship—expressed as a ratio—deviates from a specified statistical
rule, positions are taken long in the relatively undervalued security and short in the
overvalued security, with the expectation that the ratio will converge to its historical
mean. While traditional pairs trading derives returns from the cross-autocorrelation of
different stocks, our strategy focuses on the lead-lag relationship between identical
indices traded in different time zones, specifically during the high-liquidity window of
the London and New York overlap.

A growing body of research is focused on the performance of pairs trading in foreign
and specialized markets (Gatev, Goetzmann, and Rouwenhorst 2006; Perlin 2009;
Do and Faff 2010). Gatev, Goetzmann, and Rouwenhorst (2006) examined pairs
trading in the US equity market from 1962 to 2002, demonstrating that the strategy
generated an average annual return of up to 11% with low levels of risk and limited
exposure to systematic equity factors. Do and Faff (2010) extended this study, noting
that while returns remained significant, they showed a decline as market efficiency
increased. Several studies have proposed modifications to the standard
methodology, such as using Gaussian Markov chain models for the spread (Elliott,
Van Der Hoek, and Malcolm 2005) or utilizing cointegration for pair selection
(Vidyamurthy 2004). More recently, research has begun to explicitly model the
impact of transaction costs on net profitability (Do and Faff 2012).

In this paper, we make four key incremental contributions to the literature on pairs
trading. we provide extensive evidence on the profitability of a cross-border,
identical-index strategy, pairing TQQQ and LQQ3 to eliminate the idiosyncratic
company risk common in traditional equity pairs. Second, we identify a specific
"time-window alpha," focusing on the 16:00 UTC (11:00 AM NY) period when both
the London Stock Exchange and the Nasdaq are active, creating a unique
environment for price discovery. Third, we provide a comprehensive analysis of the
risks in this strategy using a multi-factor framework, specifically calculating Greeks
such as Beta and Alpha to measure market neutrality. Finally, we provide an analysis
of the strategy’s sensitivity to market frictions, incorporating price impact and bid-ask
spread estimates to evaluate the robustness of the returns.

In this study, we examine the performance of the TQQQ/LQQ3 pair from April 2024
to March 2026. Over this sample period, our results demonstrate that the strategy
delivered a cumulative total return of approximately 136%. Notably, the average
annual standard deviation for the pairs trading portfolio was significantly lower than
that of the underlying Nasdaq-100 index. These results suggest that by utilizing a
Z-score-based mean-reversion model, the strategy was able to capture significant
absolute returns during a period characterized by both falling equity prices and
spikes in volatility. Unlike previous US studies that report a drop in performance
through time ( Do and Faff 2010), our findings indicate that jurisdictional "venue
noise" continues to provide fertile ground for arbitrage.

To examine the risk exposure of the portfolio, strategy returns were regressed
against common equity factors. Our results show that the systematic risk exposure is
largely hedged, with the strategy maintaining a near-zero Beta throughout the
sample period. This confirms that the returns are generated by the closing of the
pricing gap (the ratio) rather than directional movements in the broader market. This
market-neutral characteristic makes the strategy particularly advantageous over
traditional directional investments during periods of high economic uncertainty.

Finally, we address the critical role of market frictions. Previous literature has shown
that incorporating direct transaction costs can eliminate much of the profitability in
quantitative strategies (Lesmond, Schill, and Zhou 2004; Stoll and Whaley 1983).
Our results indicate that while the TQQQ/LQQ3 pair generates robust gross returns,
the net performance is extremely sensitive to the transaction cost and bid-ask spread
applied per trade. We find that the strategy’s return can quickly be diminished by
these impacts which highlights the need for trading platforms that charge low fees.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows: Section 2 details the Z-score
and EMA methodology utilized in the study. Section 3 describes the data. Section 4
outlines the summary statistics and trading statistics. Section 5 reports the
performance characteristics, Greeks, and the impact of transaction costs, and finally,
Section 6 offers concluding remarks.

To calculate returns, this strategy first calculates the money made on the ETFs

        respectively.
This formula captures the divergence or convergence of the ratio. If the signal is 1

3. Data
This section details the source of all the data used in this paper, and how it was
processed before being used to investigate the pair trading strategy.

3.1 Data Sources

The empirical analysis uses historical market data obtained from Stooq.com, a
publicly available financial database providing historical price data for equities,
exchange-traded funds (ETFs), currencies and other financial instruments. Three
hourly datasets were downloaded covering approximately two years of observations:

   ●​   TQQQ, a US-listed 3× leveraged ETF tracking the Nasdaq-100 Index.

   ●​   LQQ3, a UK-listed 3× leveraged ETF tracking the Nasdaq-100 Index.

   ●​   The GBP/USD exchange rate.

These three datasets provide the information required to compare the pricing
behaviour of two leveraged ETFs tracking the same underlying index while
accounting for fluctuations in the exchange rate.

3.2 Data Preparation

The original datasets consisted of hourly observations. To ensure a consistent
comparison between the two ETFs, only observations recorded at 16:00 UTC were
retained. Selecting a single observation each trading day provides a consistent
reference point while capturing a period in which both the UK and US markets are
simultaneously active for much of the sample.
As TQQQ is denominated in US dollars (USD) while LQQ3 is quoted in pence
sterling (GBP), the two price series were standardised before analysis. LQQ3 prices
were first converted from pence into pounds sterling by dividing by 100 before being
converted into US dollars using the contemporaneous GBP/USD exchange rate.

During data cleansing, a small number of LQQ3 observations were identified as
being recorded using an alternative price scale (instead of pence they were recorded
in pounds). These observations were adjusted to ensure that all prices were
expressed consistently before the currency conversion was applied.

3.3 Data Alignment

Following the time filtering and currency conversion, the three datasets were merged
using their common trading date. An inner join was used so that only dates for which
observations existed across all three datasets were retained. This ensured that every
trading signal was generated from a complete and synchronised set of ETF and
foreign exchange data.

The final dataset therefore consists of one observation per trading day containing the
prices of TQQQ, the currency-adjusted prices of LQQ3, and the corresponding
GBP/USD exchange rate. These variables form the basis for constructing the
adjusted price ratio, calculating the Exponential Moving Average (EMA), rolling
standard deviation, Z-score trading signals, and all subsequent portfolio performance
and risk measures presented in this paper.



4. Initial Results
This section presents the empirical findings of the TQQQ/LQQ3 pairs trading
strategy during the sample period. To establish a baseline for performance, we first
evaluated the strategy using a fixed set of standard parameters before any advanced
optimization was applied.

4.1 Baseline Parameterization

For this initial analysis, the model’s sensitivity inputs were configured to represent a
moderate, standard mean-reversion setup. The baseline parameters were set as
follows:

   ●​   EMA Window (𝑁): 20 periods. This provides a balanced moving average that
        is responsive to recent price action without being overly sensitive to intraday
        noise.

   ●​   Entry Z-Score: 0.75. A position is initiated when the ratio deviates by
        three-quarters of a standard deviation from the mean, representing a
        moderate pricing dislocation.
   ●​   Exit Z-Score: 0.25. The position is held until the ratio reverts to within a
        quarter of a standard deviation from the mean, ensuring the bulk of the
        convergence is captured.

   ●​   Transaction Cost: 0.15% per trade. This friction accounts for the bid-ask
        spread and institutional commission structures across both the US and UK
        legs.

   ●​   Risk-Free Rate: 4.00% (Annualized). This reflects a standard baseline yield
        for short-term government treasuries during the period, used to calculate
        excess returns.

Using these baseline inputs, the strategy generated the following performance
profile.

4.2 Absolute Returns: Total and Annualized

   ●​   Total Strategy Return: 136.34% over 405 days for which clean data was
        available

   ●​   Annualized Return: 70.77%

   ●​   Annualized TQQQ Market Return (Benchmark): 29.05% during the period




The return of 136.34% demonstrates that the model successfully identified and
exploited pricing inefficiencies between the US and UK markets. Generating a return
of this magnitude over the sample period indicates that the strategy did not rely on a
few anomalous outlier events, but rather captured a consistent, high-frequency
series of spread convergences. Every time the EMA/Z-score threshold was
breached, the model effectively synthesized a profit from the structural misalignment
of the two identical indices.
When compounded and scaled to a standard 252-day trading year, the strategy
yields an Annualized Return of 70.77%. This 70.77% figure is highly significant when
viewed alongside the underlying benchmark. During the same period, simply buying
and holding the TQQQ generated an annualized return of 29.05%. The strategy
outpaced this long-only, highly directional benchmark by more than double (a spread
of over 41 percentage points). This outperformance implies that the strategy's
returns are not simply a byproduct of a broader bull market in the tech sector, but
rather the result of active, uncorrelated alpha generation.
Crucially, these returns are reported strictly net of the 0.15% transaction cost applied
to every directional change in the signal. The fact that the total return remained at
136.34% proves that, with this specific set of parameter inputs, the magnitude of the
US/UK pricing gap was wide enough—and the average profit per trade was large
enough—to aggressively overcome the persistent drag of trading fees and bid-ask
slippage.
4.3 Risk Profile: Volatility and Drawdown
   ●​ Annualized Volatility: 10.37%
   ●​ Benchmark Volatility (TQQQ): 69.17%
   ●​ Maximum Drawdown: -2.21%
To accurately evaluate the strategy's triple-digit returns, one must rigorously examine
the risk profile required to achieve them. The primary challenge in trading TQQQ and
LQQ3 is their 3x daily reset mechanism, which makes the underlying assets
inherently hyper-volatile. Typically, a long position in these leveraged ETFs exposes
an investor to extreme price swings; however, the strategy’s Annualized Volatility
was remarkably low at just 10.37%. This stands in stark contrast to the 69.17%
volatility observed in the TQQQ benchmark over the same period.
This divergence is academically significant. It indicates that the long/short
construction of the pair successfully neutralized movements of the ETFs they were
based on. By simultaneously holding long and short positions across the two
jurisdictions, the strategy effectively "cancels out" the systemic market moves,
leaving the investor exposed only to the relative pricing gap (the idiosyncratic risk).
The transition from nearly 70% volatility down to approximately 10% volatility
demonstrates the efficiency of the statistical arbitrage in consistently achieving
similar returns.
This risk reduction is further validated by the Maximum Drawdown of just -2.21%. In
the context of leveraged instruments, a peak-to-trough decline of merely 2.21% is an
exceptional result. A drawdown this shallow implies that the strategy rarely suffered
consecutive losses, and when the ratio did move against the model’s position, it did
not deviate far before mean-reverting.
From a portfolio management perspective, this "pain threshold" of approximately 2%
makes the strategy highly scalable. It suggests returns could be consistently made
without risking a catastrophic margin call or capital depletion. This level of capital
preservation is rarely seen in directional equity strategies and highlights the primary
benefit of the pairs-trading approach.

6.44% of excess return above the 4% risk-free rate baseline. This high ratio is a

Ratio
Beyond the raw numbers, a ratio this high suggests that the strategy’s equity curve is
exceptionally smooth, characterized by a steady, linear ascent with very little

the hypothesis that the strategy is capturing a persistent, structural pricing

4.5 Market Neutrality: Correlation and Beta
   ●​ Correlation to TQQQ: -0.0413
   ●​ Beta vs. TQQQ: -0.0062
The primary objective of a pairs trade is to function as a pure "Equity Market Neutral"

The Correlation coefficient of -0.0413 demonstrates that there is practically zero

moving according to the internal mechanics of the spread rather than the external

Furthermore, a Beta of -0.0062 suggests that the strategy carries no systematic

mathematical expectation for the strategy’s movement—based on market exposure
alone—would be virtually non-existent.
This lack of sensitivity implies that the profit is derived purely from the convergence
of the jurisdictional pricing gap, which protects the portfolio against broader
macroeconomic shocks, interest rate pivots, or systemic equity sell-offs. This
provides a diversification benefit as it lets it generate aggressive returns during
periods of market growth or stagnation.

5. Robustness Check and Sensitivity Analysis
A critical step in validating any algorithmic strategy is the robustness check. The goal
is to determine if the strategy’s performance is a result of a specific, narrow set of
parameters—commonly known as over-fitting—or if the underlying edge is persistent
across various configurations. By varying the EMA duration, the Z-score spread, and
the transaction costs, we can identify the strategy's breaking point and its optimal
operating range.

5.1 Sensitivity to EMA Lookback Duration

The Exponential Moving Average (EMA) serves as the strategy’s anchor, defining the
"fair value" from which the ratio deviates. We tested the strategy’s total return across
five different lookback periods to observe how the speed of the mean-reversion
signal impacts profitability.
I observed that with different EMAs we received these returns:

   ●​ EMA 10: 128.19%

   ●​   EMA 20: 136.34%

   ●​   EMA 30: 127.59%

   ●​   EMA 40: 127.58%

   ●​   EMA 50: 129.76%



The results indicate that the strategy is remarkably stable across a wide range of
lookback periods, with returns consistently hovering between 127% and 136% which
helps us alleviate fears that the strategy had been overfitting. At an EMA of 10, the
model is perhaps too reactive to short-term noise, leading to premature entries.
Conversely, as the EMA extends toward 50, the mean becomes slower to react to
structural shifts in the ratio, which slightly dampens the return. However, the fact that
even a 50-period EMA yields nearly 130% suggests that the jurisdictional spread is a
persistent phenomenon that does not require perfect timing to exploit.

5.2 Sensitivity to Z-Score Thresholds (Spread Width)

The Z-score thresholds determine the "trigger" for entering and exiting trades. A
narrow spread (0.6 entry / 0.4 exit) seeks to capture smaller, more frequent
fluctuations, while a wide spread (0.9 entry / 0.1 exit) waits for more significant
dislocations.
I observed that with different EMAs we got these returns:

   ●​ Narrow (Entry 0.6, Exit 0.4): 133.35%

   ●​   Medium (Entry 0.75, Exit 0.25): 136.34%

   ●​   Wide (Entry 0.9, Exit 0.1): 123.71%

The medium spread of 0.75/0.25 represents the optimal balance for this strategy.
The narrow configuration (133.35%) performs admirably but likely suffers from
increased transaction costs due to a higher frequency of trades. By tightening the
exit to 0.4, the strategy leaves potential profit on the table by closing positions before
the ratio has fully reverted to the mean. On the other end of the spectrum, the wide
configuration (123.71%) suffers from opportunity cost. By waiting for a 0.9 Z-score to
enter, the model misses several smaller but profitable mean-reversion events.
Furthermore, an exit at 0.1 requires a near-perfect return to the mean, which may
keep the strategy in a trade for too long, exposing it to unnecessary risk. The results
suggest that while the best strategy for jurisdictional arbitrage lies in capturing
moderate deviations that occur frequently enough to compound capital rapidly,
significant profits can be earned even when spreads are narrower or wider

5.3 Sensitivity to Transaction Costs (The Friction Limit)

Transaction costs are often very harmful for high-frequency pairs trading. We tested
the strategy against five levels of cost to find how the strategy reacted to market
frictions.
   ●​   0.00% Cost: 211.00%

   ●​   0.15% Cost: 136.34%

   ●​   0.30% Cost: 79.52%

   ●​   0.45% Cost: 36.31%

   ●​   0.75% Cost: 3.46%

This analysis reveals the strategy's extreme sensitivity to execution quality. In a
frictionless environment (0.00% cost), the strategy is hyper-profitable, returning
211%. However, as costs increase, the decay in performance is non-linear. At
0.15%, our baseline, the strategy remains highly viable at 136.34%. By the time
costs reach 0.45%, the return drops to 36.31%, which barely outperforms the
buy-and-hold TQQQ benchmark while carrying significantly more operational
complexity.

At 0.75% cost—which might represent a retail investor paying high commissions and
wide bid-ask spreads—the strategy effectively breaks, returning only 3.46%. This
shows that the TQQQ/LQQ3 arbitrage is a strategy that depends heavily on the
ability to execute trades with low slippage and minimal commissions typical of
institutions rather than retail investors. The rapid decay in returns as costs rise
highlights that the spread being captured is relatively thin, requiring a high-volume,
low-cost execution environment to remain profitable.

5.4 Robustness Conclusion
Overall, the robustness checks confirm that the strategy is statistically sound. It is not
overly dependent on a specific moving average window or a hyper-specific Z-score
entry. However, the cost of execution is a significant factor that can greatly affect
returns. So long as transaction costs remain below 0.30%, the strategy provides a
superior risk-adjusted return compared to the benchmark, validating the core thesis
of cross-border jurisdictional arbitrage.



6. Concluding Remarks
We have thoroughly analysed the profitability of a pair trading strategy between
TQQQ and LQQ3, two ETFs that track the same index but in different countries. We
have discovered that by using a mean-reversal strategy we can exploit fluctuations in
the price of the ETFs. This can lead to impressive returns which occur both in bull
and bear markets. When we analysed how robust the strategy was we found that
while factors such as the EMA duration and the Z score spread seemed to hardly
affect the strategy’s return, the amount of transaction cost was crucial with small
changes greatly changing profits. This shows that there is significant potential in
cross-country pair trading strategies, and that more research is needed to analyse
their potential profitability.
