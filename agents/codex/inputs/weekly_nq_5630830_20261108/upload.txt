This article presents the allocator-relevant metrics of a systematic weekly continuous
Nasdaq Futures (NQ, Weekly) strategy. The strategy has been tested across 26.3 years
of data with realistic transaction costs (0.07% per contract). Each metric is analyzed
against institutional thresholds, with interpretation provided for allocators.

The idea behind the “why” choosing the NASDAQ as our main asset is that, first the bias
of the index is to the upside same as for the other financial indices! Hence a long
strategy crafted - but not curve fitted - has far better higher chances than selling the
index during bear markets which are generally characterized by steady down moves
which are often erased by misleading - at times - wild up swings.

Secondly the NASDAQ components comprises issues that are growth and / or value
stocks in the heavy technology sector, also NASDAQ is excluding financials. To that end
the NASDAQ index itself is a quite forward looking or better said a leading indicator of
the US economy. Lastly since the US economy influences the most advanced world
markets so does the NASDAQ index which consequently forecasts the upcoming
economic environment for the US economy and to a lesser extend the rest advanced
economies.

Additionally, we have chosen for the long entries as execution price the next week’s
(period) open, since our studies and observations have greatly proven that this is a
quite forgiving condition. To that extend the weekly time frame (WTF) represents the
most meaningful trend of the market, hence it is the market of our choice. Moreover, the
EXIT & Stop - Loss conditions are triggered on the same signal candlestick – period with
the only exemption of the STOP-LOSS condition to exit if position lost more than 5%,
this order is executed after candlestick closes.

What does Alpha represent in this Context?

      Alpha is the excess return of the strategy compared to its benchmark (here
       the Nasdaq futures / NQ).
      It is the part of returns not explained by market beta.

In my metrics:

      Beta vs Asset = 0.1
       → means my sstrategy only moves ~10% as much as the Nasdaq itself.
      Yet my sstrategy’s CAGR = 101% (5y) versus Nasdaq’s ~ 47.9%.
       → This huge outperformance is alpha.

Namely Alpha “represents the returns the strategy generates that cannot be
explained by simply being long the market.”

Beta and Correlation are related but serve different purposes. Let’s make it crystal
clear:

1. Correlation (ρ)

      Definition: Measures the directional co-movement between two return series
       (e.g. your strategy vs Nasdaq).
      Range: –1 to +1
       • +1 = moves perfectly in sync
       • 0 = no relationship
       • –1 = moves perfectly opposite
      Meaning:
       “Does my system move when the market moves?”
       Example: my correlation ~ 0.65, my strategy often moves with Nasdaq upside
       move since we are always “long the issue”, but not perfectly.

2. Beta (β)

      Definition: Measures the magnitude of sensitivity to market moves.
      Formula:

       β = Covariance (Rp, Rm) / Variance (Rm )

       where Rp = portfolio/strategy return, Rm = market return.

      Meaning: “How much does my system move when the market moves 1%?”
       Example: my beta = 0.10, when Nasdaq rises 1%, my strategy tends to rise
       0.1%.

Key Difference.

      Correlation is the responsiveness in direction.
       (Do they move together, yes/no, and how tightly?)
      Beta is the responsiveness in magnitude.
       (How strongly does my system react when the market moves?). Low beta
       signifies that my strategy usually moves in the same direction as the market, but
       with very small amplitude (a defensive strategy). Additionally, my returns barely
       load on market risk (very low sensitivity).

That’s why allocators and trading operators would say: “It rhymes with Nasdaq trends,
but doesn’t depend on them — it produces its own alpha”.
