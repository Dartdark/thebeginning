         Abstract
         We introduce a buffer-based limit order execution framework for machine learning trading
         strategies. Our "straddle-then-reveal" approach operates on a two-day cycle: on day t, the trader
                                                  er
         enters simultaneous long and short positions at market open (Open_t); at day t's close, ML
         models—using features computed from historical data through day t—predict whether
         Close_{t+1} > Open_t; on day t+1, the "wrong" leg exits via patient limit orders placed at a
                                                pe
         buffer percentage from the intraday price extreme. Notably, anchoring the target to Open_t
         rather than Close_t proves essential: the alternative yields accuracy indistinguishable from
         random guessing, suggesting the intraday price path embeds predictive information about next-
         day movement. Testing three ML algorithms with standard and quantum-inspired feature
         selection across 48 global securities over 1,989 trading days (2018–2025), we find striking
         results. Models achieve classification accuracy near 70%, and the buffer mechanism translates
                                      ot


         this predictive power into realized profits: at the 10% buffer, average Sharpe ratios reach 14.86
         with 85.5% win rates; at the conservative 30% buffer, Sharpe ratios remain strong at 7.26 with
         65.6% win rates. The Sortino-to-Sharpe ratio ranges from 2.2× to 7.5× across configurations,
         confirming favorable return asymmetry. Robustness tests across five subperiods—including
                            tn



         the COVID-19 crisis—confirm strategy stability. Slippage sensitivity analysis demonstrates
         profitability persists across realistic transaction cost scenarios. Our findings suggest that
         execution mechanism design can be as important as prediction accuracy for trading strategy
         performance.

         1. Introduction
         The intersection of machine learning and quantitative finance has generated substantial




                                                                                    ed
         academic and practitioner interest over the past decade. While considerable research has
         focused on improving directional prediction accuracy [1-3], comparatively less attention has
         been devoted to the execution mechanism through which predictions are translated into realized
         profits. This paper addresses this gap by introducing a novel buffer-based execution framework




                                                                                 iew
         that substantially amplifies the risk-adjusted returns achievable from a given level of prediction
         accuracy.
         Traditional machine learning trading strategies follow a straightforward paradigm: predict
         direction, take position, close at predetermined exit point. This approach ties trading
         performance directly to prediction accuracy—a 60% accurate model can only generate returns
         commensurate with being correct 60% of the time. Our framework fundamentally alters this




                                                        v
         relationship through what we term the "straddle-then-reveal" approach combined with buffer-
         based limit order execution.




                                                     re
         The motivation for our approach stems from a well-documented empirical regularity in equity
         markets: intraday prices typically traverse a substantial range before settling near the close. In
         our sample of 48 global securities over 1,989 trading days, the average intraday range (high
         minus low, scaled by opening price) is 2.27%, with considerable cross-sectional variation
         ranging from 1.14% for Toyota Motor (TM) to 4.55% for Tesla (TSLA). This intraday price
                                                  er
         movement creates an opportunity for patient traders willing to place limit orders near price
         extremes [4].
                                                pe
         1.1 The Straddle-Then-Reveal Mechanism
         Our strategy operates on a two-day cycle with the following precise temporal structure. On day
         t, the trader enters both long and short positions at the market open price (Opent), creating a
         synthetic straddle with no initial directional commitment. At the close of day t, machine
         learning models generate a prediction using the complete information set available at that
                                      ot


         moment: the full price history through day t, including historical OHLCV data and technical
         indicators computed over various lookback windows (e.g., moving averages, momentum
         oscillators, volatility measures). The prediction target is whether the closing price on day t+1
         will exceed the original entry price—that is, whether Closet+1 > Opent.
                            tn



         The critical insight is that the prediction target spans across trading days: we predict the next
         day's close relative to today's open. This design ensures that (i) all information used for
         prediction—features computed over lookback windows ending on day t—is strictly available
                  rin




         at the time the forecast is made (end of day t), and (ii) the trader has a full trading day (t+1) to
         execute the exit strategy based on the prediction.
         On day t+1, armed with the end-of-day-t prediction, the trader acts as a "patient trader" [4]:
         limit orders are placed to exit the "wrong" leg—the position contra to the predicted direction—
        ep




         at favorable prices near the intraday extreme, while the "kept" leg is held until the market close
         of day t+1. Table 1 summarizes this temporal sequence.

         This sequencing—position first, prediction second, execution third—addresses a fundamental

         challenge in applied machine learning trading: the gap between theoretical prediction accuracy
         and realized execution. By entering both directions simultaneously at Opent, we eliminate
         timing risk at entry. By delaying the directional commitment until information is fully
         available, we ensure the prediction uses only observable data with no look-ahead bias. By using
         limit orders for the wrong leg exit on day t+1, we capture a portion of the intraday range that
                                                  er
         would otherwise be surrendered to immediacy costs.

         The Information Value of Intraday Observation
                                                pe
         A natural question arises: why anchor the prediction target to Opent rather than Closet? We
         investigated this alternative specification—predicting whether Closet+1 > Closet—and found
         that classification accuracy collapsed to approximately 50%, statistically indistinguishable
         from random guessing. This striking contrast reveals the economic logic underlying our
         approach.
                                      ot


         The intraday price path on day t—from Opent through Closet—embeds information about order
         flow, liquidity dynamics, and market sentiment that proves predictive of the following day's
         directional movement relative to the starting point of that information revelation process. By
         entering the straddle at Opent and observing the complete day-t session before committing
                            tn



         directionally, we allow the market's price discovery mechanism to inform our prediction. The
         ML models effectively learn to interpret what day t's trading 'meant' for day t+1's trajectory.
         This finding has a practical implication that reinforces our framework: the trader must be
                  rin




         positioned before the informative price path unfolds. Waiting until Closet to enter would
         sacrifice precisely the information that makes prediction possible. The straddle structure is
         therefore not merely a hedging device but an information acquisition mechanism—the cost of
         holding offsetting positions during day t is the price paid for observing the market's revelation
         process from within.
        ep




         1.2 The Buffer Innovation
         The key innovation of our framework is the buffer-based execution mechanism for the wrong
         leg. Rather than exiting at market prices (which would result in losses when the prediction is
Pr




         correct), we place limit orders at a predetermined percentage—the "buffer"—from the intraday
         price extreme. Since we cannot know the exact high or low in advance, the buffer serves as a
         safety margin: a smaller buffer (e.g., 10%) places orders closer to the expected extreme,

         capturing more of the range but with higher execution risk; a larger buffer (e.g., 30%) sacrifices
         some potential profit for higher fill probability.

         2. Literature Review
         This section reviews five interconnected literature streams that inform our research: machine




                                                     re
         learning in financial prediction, market microstructure and execution algorithms,
         simultaneously long-short trading strategies, intraday price dynamics, and performance
         evaluation methodology. We conclude by identifying the research gap that motivates our

                                                  er
         2.1 Machine Learning in Financial Prediction
         The application of machine learning to financial prediction has evolved substantially over three
         decades. Early work by Kaastra and Boyd [6] and Tsaih, Hsu, and Lai [7] demonstrated the
                                                pe
         potential of neural networks for forecasting stock prices and market direction. These
         foundational studies established that nonlinear models could capture patterns invisible to
         traditional linear approaches, though they also highlighted challenges related to overfitting and
         non-stationarity.
         The introduction of ensemble methods marked a significant advance. Breiman2001's [8]
                                      ot


         Random Forest algorithm addressed overfitting through bagging and feature randomization,
         making it particularly suitable for high-dimensional financial data. Friedman [9] developed
         gradient boosting, which sequentially builds trees to correct predecessors' errors. Chen and
         Guestrin [10] introduced XGBoost, combining gradient boosting with regularization and
                            tn



         computational efficiency, rapidly becoming the dominant algorithm for tabular financial data.
         Recent comprehensive studies have established machine learning's empirical validity. Gu,
         Kelly, and Xiu [1] provide landmark evidence that ML methods—particularly neural networks
         and tree-based ensembles—generate economically significant out-of-sample predictability in
                  rin




         the cross-section of stock returns. Their analysis of thousands of predictive signals
         demonstrates that ML substantially outperforms traditional linear models. Krauss et al. [3]
         apply deep neural networks, gradient-boosted trees, and random forests to statistical arbitrage
         on S&P 500 constituents, achieving significant risk-adjusted returns. Fischer and Krauss [11]
         extend this work using LSTM networks for directional prediction.
        ep




         For comprehensive reviews, Henrique, Sobreiro, and Kimura [12] survey over 100 papers
         applying ML to market prediction, identifying gradient boosting and neural networks as
         dominant approaches. Sezer, Gudelek, and Ozbayoglu [13] provide a systematic review of deep
         learning applications, documenting the shift from traditional technical analysis to data-driven
Pr




         feature learning. Zhang, Zohren, and Roberts [14] examine deep learning specifically for
         portfolio optimization.




This preprint research paper has not been peer reviewed. Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=6401038
         Limitation: Despite impressive advances in prediction accuracy, this literature implicitly
         assumes frictionless execution—that predicted directions can be instantaneously monetized at




                                                                                    ed
         observed prices. The gap between prediction and profitable execution remains largely
         unaddressed. A model achieving 55% directional accuracy does not automatically generate
         55% winning trades when transaction costs, slippage, and execution timing are considered.

         2.2 Market Microstructure and Execution Algorithms




                                                                                 iew
         Market microstructure theory provides the foundation for understanding trade execution. Kyle
         [15] models how informed traders strategically time their trades to minimize price impact,
         establishing that larger orders move prices adversely. Glosten and Milgrom [16] explain bid-
         ask spreads as compensation for adverse selection risk faced by market makers trading with
         potentially informed counterparties. These models establish that execution is costly and
         strategically important.




                                                        v
         Glosten [4] formalizes the distinction between "patient traders" who supply liquidity by posting




                                                     re
         limit orders and "urgent traders" who demand immediacy through market orders. Patient
         traders earn the bid-ask spread but face non-execution risk; urgent traders pay the spread for
         guaranteed execution. This framework is central to our buffer-based approach: by placing limit
         orders near intraday extremes, we act as patient traders harvesting the intraday range.
         The optimal execution literature, pioneered by Bertsimas and Lo [17] and Almgren and Chriss
                                                  er
         [18], develops frameworks for minimizing execution costs when liquidating large positions.
         These models trade off market impact against timing risk, typically solving for optimal
         execution trajectories. Nevmyvaka, Feng, and Kearns [19] apply reinforcement learning to
                                                pe
         optimize execution, demonstrating that ML can improve upon analytical solutions.
         Empirically, Frazzini, Israel, and Moskowitz [20] document that patient limit order strategies
         achieve substantially lower execution costs than aggressive market orders. Using proprietary
         data from a large institutional investor, they show that the gap between paper returns and
         realized returns is smaller for patient execution strategies. For comprehensive treatments of
         market microstructure, see Hasbrouck [21], O'Hara [22], and Harris [23].
                                      ot


         Limitation: The execution literature optimizes trade implementation but largely ignores
         directional prediction. The focus is on minimizing costs for a given order, not on generating
         trading signals. While Nevmyvaka et al. [19] apply ML to execution, the objective remains
                            tn



         cost minimization rather than alpha generation. The potential for execution mechanism design
         to amplify predictive signals remains unexplored.

         2.3 Simultaneously Long-Short Trading Strategies
                  rin




         The Simultaneously Long-Short (SLS) framework, developed in the control theory literature,
         demonstrates that maintaining both long and short positions can generate positive expected
         returns even without forecasting ability. Barmish and Primbs [24] show that in an idealized
         Brownian motion market, a linear feedback controller maintaining simultaneous positions can
         achieve positive expected profit through what they term "volatility pumping." Barmish and
        ep




         Primbs [25] extend this to a model-free feedback controller paradigm, demonstrating
         robustness to model misspecification.
         Baumann and Grüne [26] analyze SLS trading in both discrete and continuous time,
         establishing theoretical conditions under which simultaneous positioning generates positive
Pr




         returns. Their analysis shows that the key mechanism is the ability to adjust positions as prices
         move, capturing gains from volatility rather than directional prediction.




This preprint research paper has not been peer reviewed. Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=6401038
         Related work on pairs trading [27] and statistical arbitrage [3] employs long-short positioning
         but with different objectives—exploiting relative mispricing rather than volatility harvesting.




                                                                                    ed
         These strategies typically maintain market neutrality across correlated securities rather than
         holding opposing positions in the same security.
         Limitation: The SLS literature relies on continuous feedback control—adjusting positions

         trading, generating substantial transaction costs in practice. Moreover, the framework operates

         2.4 Intraday Price Dynamics and Range-Based Volatility
         The literature on intraday price dynamics establishes empirical regularities that our strategy




                                                        v
         exploits. Parkinson [28] demonstrates that the high-low range provides a more efficient
         volatility estimator than close-to-close returns, as the range captures intraday variation




                                                     re

         open, high, low, and close prices, showing that incorporating intraday extremes substantially
         improves estimation precision.

         robust to microstructure noise than return-based measures. They demonstrate that the log range
                                                  er
         follows an approximately normal distribution, enabling tractable modeling. Importantly for our
         approach, their analysis confirms that intraday prices reliably traverse a substantial range—

                                                pe

         been documented. Wood, McInish, and Ord [31] and Harris [32] find U-shaped intraday
         volatility patterns with elevated activity at open and close. These patterns suggest that
         overnight information is impounded at the open, followed by intraday price discovery that
         typically reverses portions of the opening move.
                                      ot


         Limitation: The intraday dynamics literature focuses primarily on volatility estimation and
         price discovery rather than trading strategy design. While the empirical regularities—
         substantial intraday ranges, mean-reverting opening gaps—are well documented, their
         exploitation through structured trading mechanisms remains underdeveloped. The observation
                            tn



         that prices traverse predictable ranges invites the question: can this range be systematically
         harvested?

         2.6 Research Gap and Contribution
         While each literature stream has advanced our understanding of its respective domain, a critical
         gap remains at their intersection. The machine learning literature optimizes prediction accuracy
                                                  er
         but assumes frictionless execution—ignoring the substantial gap between predicted and
         realized returns. The microstructure literature optimizes execution but lacks predictive
         signals—focusing on cost minimization rather than alpha generation. The SLS literature uses
                                                pe
         simultaneous positioning but relies on continuous feedback control—requiring high-frequency
         trading and ignoring discrete prediction opportunities. The intraday dynamics literature
         documents exploitable patterns but stops short of designing trading mechanisms—leaving the
         practical question of range harvesting unanswered.
         No study integrates machine learning directional prediction with a buffer-based limit
         order execution mechanism within a straddle framework. This gap is consequential: as we
                                      ot


         demonstrate empirically, execution mechanism design can amplify returns substantially
         beyond what prediction accuracy alone would suggest. A model with random directional
         accuracy (50%) generates Sharpe ratios of 4.6 when combined with our buffer-based execution;
                            tn



         machine learning predictions (71% accuracy) amplify this seven-fold to Sharpe ratios
         exceeding 19—a synergy that neither the ML trading literature nor the execution literature has
         previously documented.
         Our contribution bridges these literatures. From ML, we adopt ensemble classification methods
                  rin




         for directional prediction. From microstructure, we employ the patient trader framework, using
         limit orders to capture intraday range. From SLS, we borrow simultaneous positioning at
         entry—entering both long and short without initial directional commitment. From the intraday

        ep




         measures.

         risk. By using buffers for wrong-leg exits, we convert directional predictions—even imperfect

         We employ a comprehensive suite of performance metrics spanning risk-adjusted returns,

         Omega, Sortino, Calmar) framework advocated by López de Prado [35], supplemented with
         probabilistic measures from Bailey and López de Prado [5] and our own contribution extending

                                                  er
         3.8.1 Risk-Adjusted Return Metrics
         Sharpe Ratio [42] measures excess return per unit of total risk:
                                                pe
                                               SR = (R̄ - R_f) / σ    (8)
         where R̄ is mean return, R_f is the risk-free rate (assumed zero for daily returns), and σ is return
         standard deviation. We report annualized Sharpe ratios computed as SR_annual = SR_daily ×
         √252. A Sharpe ratio exceeding 2.0 is generally considered excellent for traditional strategies.
         Sortino Ratio [38] replaces total volatility with downside deviation, addressing the criticism
                                      ot


         that Sharpe ratio penalizes upside volatility equally with downside:
                                             SoR = (R̄ - R_f) / σ_d    (9)
         where σ_d = √(E[min(R - R_f, 0)²]) is the downside deviation, computed only over returns
                            tn



         falling below the threshold. This metric is particularly appropriate when return distributions
         are asymmetric, as it does not penalize favorable volatility. For strategies with truncated
         downside—such as our buffer-based approach—the Sortino ratio provides a more accurate
         assessment of risk-adjusted performance than the Sharpe ratio.
                  rin




         Calmar Ratio relates annualized return to maximum drawdown, capturing the return generated
         per unit of peak-to-trough decline:
                                       Calmar = R_annual / |MaxDD|           (10)
         where MaxDD = max_t(max_{s≤t}(V_s) - V_t) / max_{s≤t}(V_s) is the maximum peak-to-
        ep




         trough decline in portfolio value V. The Calmar ratio is particularly relevant for strategies
         where drawdown management is a primary concern. A Calmar ratio exceeding 2.0 indicates
         that annualized returns are at least twice the worst experienced drawdown.
Pr

         Traditional performance metrics provide point estimates without quantifying estimation
         uncertainty. Bailey and López de Prado (2012, 2014) develop probabilistic extensions that

         estimate the probability of genuine skill versus luck.
         Probabilistic Sharpe Ratio (PSR) estimates the probability that the true Sharpe ratio exceeds
         a benchmark SR*:
                                        PSR = Φ[(SR̂ - SR*) / σ̂(SR̂)]      (11)




                                                                                    iew
         where Φ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function and the standard error of the
         Sharpe ratio estimator accounts for non-normality:
                              σ̂(SR̂) = √[(1 - γ₃×SR̂ + (γ₄-1)/4 × SR̂²) / (n-1)]     (12)
         where γ₃ is skewness, γ₄ is kurtosis, and n is sample size. Setting SR* = 0, the PSR estimates
         the probability that returns are genuinely positive rather than a result of sampling variation.




                                                        v
         This adjustment is critical for financial returns, which typically exhibit fat tails (high kurtosis)

         3.8.3 Trade Quality Metrics
         Profit Factor measures the ratio of gross profits to gross losses, providing a simple indicator
                                      ot


         of strategy profitability:
                                 PF = Σ(R_t | R_t > 0) / |Σ(R_t | R_t < 0)|         (14)
                            tn



         A Profit Factor exceeding 1.0 indicates profitability; values above 2.0 are considered strong,
         indicating that gross profits are at least twice gross losses. An infinite Profit Factor occurs when
         there are no losing periods—all returns are positive.
         Omega Ratio [47] compares probability-weighted gains to losses relative to a threshold τ:
                  rin




                                 Ω(τ) = ∫_τ^∞ [1-F(r)]dr / ∫_{-∞}^τ F(r)dr          (15)
         where F is the cumulative distribution function of returns. Unlike the Sharpe ratio, which
         summarizes the return distribution using only mean and variance, the Omega ratio captures the
         entire distribution without assuming normality. Setting τ = 0, the Omega ratio compares the
        ep




         probability-weighted area of gains to losses. An Omega ratio exceeding 1.0 indicates positive
         expected returns; values above 2.0 suggest strongly favorable risk-reward characteristics.

         term geometric growth:

         where p is win probability, q = 1-p is loss probability, and b is the win/loss ratio (average win

         3.8.4 Downside Risk Metrics
         Value-at-Risk (VaR) at confidence level α quantifies the maximum expected loss under
         normal market conditions:




                                                                                    iew
                                      VaR_α = -inf{r : P(R ≤ r) ≥ 1-α}      (17)
         Interpreted: VaR_95% is the loss level exceeded only 5% of the time. We compute VaR using
         historical simulation—the empirical quantile of realized returns—which makes no
         distributional assumptions. We report VaR at both 95% and 99% confidence levels.
         Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) , also known as Expected Shortfall, measures the average
         loss conditional on exceeding the VaR threshold:




                                                        v
                                       CVaR_α = -E[R | R ≤ -VaR_α]          (18)




                                                     re
         CVaR addresses a limitation of VaR: VaR indicates how bad losses can get under normal
         conditions but says nothing about the severity of losses in the tail. CVaR provides the expected
         loss when things go wrong, making it more informative for risk management. CVaR is also
         coherent in the mathematical sense, satisfying subadditivity—a property VaR lacks.

         3.8.5 Probabilistic Sortino Ratio: A Methodological Contribution
         We extend Bailey and López de Prado's PSR framework to the Sortino ratio, addressing
         a gap in the performance evaluation literature. While PSR accounts for non-normality
         through skewness and kurtosis adjustments, it does not distinguish between desirable upside
                                                pe
         volatility and undesirable downside volatility. For strategies with asymmetric return profiles—
         where the goal is to maximize upside while truncating downside—a probabilistic measure
         focused on downside risk is more appropriate.
         The Probabilistic Sortino Ratio (PSoR) estimates the probability that the true Sortino ratio
         exceeds a benchmark:
                                      ot


                                     PSoR = Φ[(SoR̂ - SoR*) / σ̂(SoR̂)]      (19)
         The standard error of the Sortino ratio estimator requires adjustment for the asymmetric
         treatment of returns. Because downside deviation is computed only over negative returns, the
                            tn



         effective sample size is reduced and the sampling distribution differs from that of the Sharpe
         ratio:
                               σ̂(SoR̂) = √[(1 + SoR̂²/2 - γ₃⁻×SoR̂) / (n_d - 1)]   (20)
                  rin




         where n_d is the number of negative return observations (returns below the threshold) and γ₃⁻
         is the skewness of the negative return distribution. This formulation accounts for two effects:
         (1) the reduced effective sample size when using only downside observations, and (2) the
         asymmetric distribution of downside returns, which may differ substantially from the full return
         distribution.
        ep




         The PSoR is particularly appropriate for our buffer-based strategy, which by design truncates
         downside through favorable wrong-leg exits. The Sortino-to-Sharpe ratio for our strategy
         ranges from 2.2 to 7.5 depending on buffer level, indicating substantial return asymmetry.
         Under such conditions, the Sharpe ratio understates risk-adjusted performance by penalizing
Pr




         favorable upside volatility, while the PSR inherits this limitation. The PSoR provides a
         probabilistic assessment of strategy quality that appropriately focuses on the downside risk that
         investors actually seek to minimize.

         This paper introduces a novel buffer-based limit order execution framework that fundamentally
         alters the relationship between prediction accuracy and trading profitability. Our "straddle-

         Our findings carry several implications for theory and future research:
                                                  er

                                                pe

         accuracy via the rescue effect and conservative buffers accepting slightly lower win rates in

         forecasting models should be complemented by equal attention to execution frameworks that
         interact with prediction value.

         For market microstructure, we provide evidence that patient limit order strategies can
         systematically extract value from intraday price dynamics. The Glosten [4] distinction between
         patient and urgent traders takes on practical significance: our buffer mechanism formalizes
         patient trading as a profit source rather than merely a cost reduction technique. The finding that
                            tn



         wrong-leg profitability depends on buffer aggressiveness suggests an optimal patience level
         that balances range capture against execution probability.
         For the simultaneously long-short literature, we extend the SLS framework from continuous
         feedback control to discrete ML-based prediction. Our "straddle-then-reveal" approach—
                  rin




         entering simultaneous positions then using predictions to determine which leg to keep—

         positioning is feasible.

         7.4 Limitations and Caveats
         Several limitations warrant acknowledgment and should inform interpretation of our results:
         Execution assumption: Our backtest assumes limit order execution if prices touch the
                                      ot


         specified level during the trading day. In practice, limit orders may not fill if the touch is brief,
         volume at the limit price is exhausted, or queue priority is unfavorable. Live trading would
         require empirical validation of fill rates. The 30% buffer partially mitigates this concern by
                            tn



         targeting more achievable price levels.
         Market impact: Our analysis assumes positions are small enough to not impact prices. For
         larger portfolios, the act of placing limit orders near extremes could affect intraday dynamics,
         particularly for less liquid securities. Capacity constraints likely exist, though our focus on
         highly liquid securities mitigates this concern.
                  rin




         Short sale constraints: The strategy requires simultaneous long and short positioning, which
         may be constrained for some investors. Short sale costs (borrow fees) are not explicitly
         modeled; for hard-to-borrow securities, these costs could materially impact returns. Restricting
         to easy-to-borrow securities addresses this concern.
        ep




         Sample period: Our 2018-2025 sample includes generally favorable equity market conditions.
         Performance during extended bear markets (e.g., 2008-2009) remains untested. The COVID-
         19 analysis provides some evidence of crash resilience, but caution is warranted in
         extrapolating to different crisis types.
Pr




         Replication risk: Widespread adoption of this strategy could reduce its effectiveness if many
         participants target similar limit order levels, compressing available returns. This is a common
         concern for published strategies and suggests ongoing monitoring of strategy decay.

         7.6 Interpretation Through Market Microstructure
         Our finding that even random predictions combined with buffer execution generate positive
         returns (Sharpe ratio of 4.6) might appear to challenge market efficiency. However, we
                            tn



         interpret this result through the lens of market microstructure: the buffer mechanism effectively
         positions the trader as a liquidity provider, earning compensation for patience and execution
         risk—consistent with Glosten1994's [4] patient trader framework. The ML prediction
         component amplifies this base return (Sharpe 4.6 → 19.3) by intelligently selecting which
                  rin




         position to maintain. Thus, our framework represents a synthesis of microstructure-based
         execution and predictive modeling, rather than a direct challenge to market efficiency. The
         intraday range—documented by Parkinson [28] and Garman and Klass [29]—provides the
         "fuel" that the buffer mechanism harvests, while prediction accuracy determines how
         efficiently that fuel is converted into returns.
